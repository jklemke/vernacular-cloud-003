concept: norm

Definition

The why behind the why. Per Brandom the norms are a commitment to coherence, that is

compatibility and incompatibility, that is an obligation to ask for and to give reasons.

norms are not primarily discursive rules

they are pre-suppositional constraints

they sort outcomes (good/bad, better/worse)

they are approached pragmatically, not constituted linguistically

But for Badinage the norms are a commitment to survival and to thriving; then reality emerges as a secondary norm, a commitment to truth as foundation-correspondence-coherence.

The norm of reality is a preconditions of survival and flourishing.

The norm of truth is a precondition of ostensive language.

The norm of sentience is survival; the norm of sapience is truth. To be sentient is to act in accordance with the norm of survival; to be sapient is to act in accordance with the norm of truth.

Normative sapient practice is supervenient on the norms of sentient and social practice. I agree with much of Brandom's philosophical approach, but I think it is in the notion of norms where I may diverge from Brandom. Is see norms as preconditions for practice. For example, survival, flourishing, and the assumption of stable, regular patterns in external reality are preconditional norms of sentient practice. The notion of truth is, likewise, a preconditional norm of sapient descriptive practice.

JWK

This is where the sidelined discussion on determinism enters the picture. I have a non-reductionist, supervenient-emergentist view of nature. Terrence Deacon is an ally here, I think. Also George F R Ellis, though he has a tendency to go off onto tangents.

Though I am a naturalist, I don't follow Sean Carroll in viewing emergence as a "way of talking". That is too close to Brandom, Rorty, and Huw Price.

I think volcanoes, stars, and whirlpools for example are ontological existence causal systems that cannot be reduced to basic physics. Where I think I agree with Carroll is that all the higher level emergence is supervenient on basic forces of physics. But the ontological cause happens at the system level of star, volcano, glacier, river, atmospheric storm, etc.

So emergence doesn't introduce any new basic forces. But friction, heat transfer, pressure, orbital paths, centers of gravity expand the notion of "causal closure". To put it poetically, the star moves the atoms, the atoms don't move the star.

Where Terrence Deacon comes in with abiogenesis. Non-living nature exhibits structural functional organization in a non-reductive way, but it is non-normative. The first norm is survival. Without life there are no norms. Survival emerges (details to be determined, but generally shape is understood). Further norms like flourishing and sociality emerge from survival.

The next great boundary is the emergence of sapient practice. Truth is a norm that is co-evolves with the evolution of fully grammatical language. Legitimacy co-evolves with the evolution of cultural grounded social institutions.

You’ve now got a very clear backbone: a **layered, emergentist account of normativity** tied to real transitions in nature. And you’re right that bringing in emergence (in a robust, not merely “as-if”) sense is what lets you resist both reductionism _and_ discursive idealism.

Let me organize and stress-test what you’re proposing, especially in relation to people like Terrence Deacon, George F. R. Ellis, and Sean Carroll.

---

1) Brandom: norms as socially articulated commitments

For Brandom:

  • norms = **statuses** (commitment, entitlement)
  • instituted by **social practices**
  • articulated through **giving and asking for reasons**

So:

> normativity = participation in a rule-governed, discursive game

---

Why this feels too narrow to you

Because it implies:

  • no normativity in:
  • perception
  • skill
  • animal cognition
  • no normativity without:
  • language
  • explicit accountability

You’re resisting that by saying:

> norms are prior to, and govern, those practices—not created by them

That’s a deep disagreement about _ontological priority_.

---

2) Pragmatism (Peirce / Lewis via Misak)

Charles Sanders Peirce

C. I. Lewis

Cheryl Misak

This is much closer to your stance.

Key idea:

  • truth is a **norm regulating inquiry**
  • not reducible to:
  • consensus
  • utility
  • immediate success

Misak’s reading emphasizes:

> inquiry aims at truth, even though we never fully secure it

---

Where you extend this

You’re adding:

> the norm of truth operates already at the level of sentient corroboration

That’s not explicit in Peirce, but it’s a natural extension.

You’re also pluralizing:

  • truth is one norm among others:
  • survival
  • flourishing
  • coherence (implicitly)
  • legitimacy

So you’re building:

> a **multi-norm constraint field**

3) Enactivism: norms as viability constraints

Francisco Varela

Evan Thompson

Here, “norm” is not linguistic at all.

Norm = viability condition

  • viable vs non-viable states

Strong overlap with your view

Your list:

  • survival
  • flourishing

maps directly onto:

> viability norms

And crucially:

---

Where you go further

Enactivism often avoids:

  • truth as a robust norm

You insist:

> truth is also a norm, not reducible to viability

That’s an important addition.

4) Predictive processing: norms as error minimization

Karl Friston

Implicitly:

  • worse models → higher error

You would say:

  • error minimization = one **dimension (correspondence-like)**

  • coherence
  • broader norms (truth, legitimacy, meaning)

So you resist:

> reducing normativity to a single metric

---

5) Developmental psychology: norms as learning constraints

Alison Gopnik

Children:

  • distinguish:
  • correct vs incorrect
  • expected vs violated
  • revise models

---

Key implication

Normativity shows up as:

> sensitivity to error, correction, and improvement—before language

This strongly supports your position that:

> normativity is not coextensive with discursive reasoning

---

6) Ecological psychology: norms as attunement

James J. Gibson

  • environment offers **affordances**
  • organisms are more or less **attuned**

So:

> normativity = better or worse attunement to real structure

---

This maps onto your “correspondence”

  • not representational accuracy in a strict sense
  • but **fit between organism and world**

7) Pulling this together: your emerging theory of norms

Across these frameworks, we get different models:

|Framework|What is a norm?|

|---|---|

|Brandom|discursive commitment|

|Pragmatism|regulative ideal of inquiry|

|Enactivism|viability constraint|

|Predictive processing|error minimization|

|Developmental psych|learning constraint|

Your synthesis (this is the key insight)

You are implicitly proposing:

> norms are **multi-level constraints that govern the evaluation of outcomes across biological, cognitive, and discursive systems**

More specifically:

  • **foundation** → viability / embodiment
  • **correspondence** → attunement / prediction success
  • **coherence** → systemic compatibility
  • **truth** → regulative norm of inquiry

8) Your definition of norm (important)

You said:

> a norm is a pre-suppositional condition that governs behaviour and sorts good outcomes from bad

That’s quite powerful, but also raises a key issue:

---

The challenge

What distinguishes:

  • a **norm**

from

  • a **mere constraint or regularity**?

Example:

  • gravity “sorts” outcomes (you fall or don’t)
  • but we don’t usually call it a norm

---

You likely want something like:

> norms are constraints that are _evaluatively loaded_ within a system of activity

i.e.:

  • not just causal
  • but tied to:
  • success/failure
  • better/worse
  • improvement

---

9) Your “apophatic” remark (very interesting)

You said norms are:

> meta-linguistic, even apophatic

That suggests:

  • norms are not fully capturable in propositions
  • they are **conditions of intelligibility and evaluation**

This is very close to:

  • Kant (conditions of possibility)
  • but also:
  • later Wittgenstein (rules shown in practice)

Bottom line

You are converging on a position where:

> Norms are not created by discourse, nor reducible to biology, but are **multi-level constraints that govern the evaluation of activity across organism, cognition, and society**.

And:

> Truth is one such norm—irreducible, not constructed, but approached through practices of corroboration.

This lets you say, against Brandom:

  • giving and asking for reasons is **in the service of norms**
  • not the **source of normativity itself**

If you want to push this further, the next crucial step is:

👉 to give a **clean criterion that distinguishes norms from mere causal constraints**

—that’s the point at which your view becomes fully defensible against both:

  • reductive naturalism
  • and discursive idealism.

1) Your core metaphysical picture (cleaned up)

You’re proposing a **stratified ontology with supervenience + real emergence**:

Level 1: Physical systems (non-normative)

  • stars, volcanoes, rivers, storms
  • real causal organization at system level
  • not reducible _in practice_ to microphysics
  • but still supervenient on it

Level 2: Living systems (first norm)

  • emergence of **survival**
  • teleology enters (in a naturalized sense)
  • behavior becomes evaluable: viable / non-viable

Level 3: Social / cognitive organisms

  • **flourishing**, **sociality**
  • learning, coordination, culture

Level 4: Sapient/discursive practice

  • **truth** (norm of inquiry)
  • **legitimacy** (norm of institutions)

---

This is a strong claim:

> Normativity is not everywhere—it _enters_ the world with life.

That’s a clear break from:

  • Brandom (normativity = discursive)
  • pan-normative views (norms everywhere)
  • reductive naturalism (norms nowhere)

---

2) Your emergence claim vs Carroll

Sean Carroll

Carroll’s view (roughly):

  • emergence = useful description
  • all causation is fundamentally microphysical
  • higher-level talk = “way of talking”

---

Your disagreement

You’re saying:

Your line:

> “the star moves the atoms, the atoms don’t move the star”

That’s a poetic way of asserting:

  • or at least **system-level causal autonomy**

Where you still agree with Carroll

  • no new fundamental forces
  • supervenience holds

So your position is:

> **non-reductive physicalism with real emergence**

> life → **normativity (survival as first norm)**

That’s a small but decisive step.

  • causation is multi-level

> **Normativity begins with life (survival), not with language.**

|Domain|Status|

|---|---|

6) Why this avoids the main traps

(A) Avoids reductionism

Norms are not:

  • eliminable
  • or “just talk”

They emerge at a real level.

---

(B) Avoids Brandom-style discursive idealism

Norms are not:

  • created by language

They are:

> articulated by language

---

(C) Avoids crude teleology

You don’t posit:

  • cosmic purpose

Only:

  • organism-relative norms (survival, flourishing)

7) The pressure point: are norms just functions?

Here’s where your view will be challenged.

A critic might say:

> “survival” is just biological function, not a norm

So the key question becomes:

What makes survival _normative_ rather than just functional?

---

Your likely answer (needs to be explicit)

Something like:

> A norm exists where a system’s organization makes outcomes evaluable as better or worse relative to its continued existence and activity.

That is:

  • not just causal
  • but **teleologically structured**
  • and **error-sensitive**

---

8) The big transition: survival → truth

This is your most ambitious step:

> truth emerges with language

---

Why this is plausible

Language enables:

  • explicit claims
  • disagreement
  • justification
  • error recognition at a higher level

So:

> truth = norm governing _discursive corroboration_

---

But note the tension

You also want:

> sentient practice is already corroborational

So you’ll need to distinguish:

  • **proto-corroboration (pre-linguistic)**
  • **truth-directed corroboration (linguistic)**

Otherwise it looks like:

  • truth is already there before language

---

9) Your strongest formulation (refined)

Here’s a tightened version of your overall view:

> Nature exhibits a hierarchy of emergent organizational levels, each introducing new forms of constraint and evaluation. Non-living systems exhibit causal organization without normativity. With the emergence of life, normativity enters the world in the form of survival: behavior becomes evaluable as viable or non-viable relative to the organism’s continued existence. More complex organisms introduce further norms such as flourishing and social coordination. With the emergence of language, normativity becomes explicit and reflexive, giving rise to the norms of truth and legitimacy that govern discursive and institutional practices.

---

10) Bottom line

You’re developing a position that:

  • takes emergence seriously (against Sean Carroll)
  • grounds normativity in biology (with Terrence Deacon)
  • allows multi-level causation (with George F. R. Ellis)
  • but extends all of them into a **normative ontology**

The key claim is:

> norms are not linguistic inventions or mere descriptions—they are real features of organized systems that arise with life and develop into increasingly explicit forms.

---

If you want to sharpen this further, the next critical step is:

👉 to clearly distinguish **function**, **teleology**, and **normativity**

—that’s where most objections will concentrate, especially from both philosophers and scientists.
