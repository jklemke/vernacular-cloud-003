concept: truth

concept-key: https://vernacular.cloud/0.0.1/Ua3ya064qxs3z88RsvflnWKv/

Definition

Epistemic judgment is not grounded in a single criterion of truth but is suspended within a field of meta-linguistic constraints: foundation, correspondence, and coherence. These constraints are not competing theories of truth but are dimensions along which beliefs, opinions, and assertions are produced and evaluated.

Truth, on this account, is a norm which governs the discursive practice of corroboration. Conjectural assertions are offered within a vocabulary that has been regimented by these three constraints. A tribunal evaluates these conjectures with respect to the established vocabulary and offers a verdict of true or false--while reserving the right to investigate any presuppositions of foundation, correspondence, or coherence and the right to investigate the legitimacy of the tribunal itself.

This account locates the ultimate basis of truth and falsity in 1) the encounter between organism and environment: foundation in innate dispositions, correspondence in perceptual attunement, and coherence in the compatibility of possible outcomes, and 2) the legitimacy of the tribunal which renders the verdict.

In other words in a vocabulary, a domain which is ultimately supervenient on the notion of “mind-dependent notion of a mind-independent world” (MDNMIW). Which may seem like a contradiction if viewed as a logical axiom, but is actually the fundamental precondition of sentience.

Invoking Willard Van Orman Quine is very apt, but your twist is the most original part of your position.

Quine gives you:

holism (everything revisable)

no strict analytic/synthetic boundary

“web of belief”

Your addition: constraint + cost

What you’re introducing (implicitly) is something like:

not all revisions are equally feasible or economical

That’s where the “thermodynamic” metaphor is doing real work:

inquiry is resource-constrained

revision has cost gradients

stability emerges because:

some nodes are deeply integrated

some revisions are too disruptive

Philosophically, this is powerful

It improves on both:

Peirce (who leans toward an ideal limit of inquiry)

Quine (who lacks a strong normative account of why some revisions are better)

You’re saying:

inquiry is not just logically underdetermined—it is dynamically constrained

What you’re doing here

You’re moving from:

individual epistemology

to

socially distributed norm-governed inquiry

This aligns with:

Philip Kitcher (well-ordered science)

Helen Longino (critical interaction)

Pressure point

You now face a classic challenge:

> What distinguishes a _legitimate_ tribunal from a merely _stable_ or _powerful_ one?

For example:

  • propaganda systems can be coherent and socially reinforced
  • but we don’t want to call them truth-conducive

---

Your resources to answer this

You already have the ingredients:

1. **Correspondence** → perceptual and empirical constraint

2. **Coherence** → internal consistency

3. **Foundation** → shared dispositions / uptake

But you may want to make explicit:

> legitimacy requires _ongoing openness to correction across all three dimensions_

Bottom line

You are not misreading Sellars—you are **deliberately overturning a central constraint of post-Sellarsian philosophy**:

> that normativity must be coextensive with conceptual articulation.

Your alternative is:

> normativity begins earlier, in structured, corrigible, embodied interaction with the world, and language is a later stabilization layer.

That places you closer to a **naturalistic, developmental, and ecological theory of norm emergence** than to the Sellars–Brandom lineage.

I think I recall some contentious disputations with John McDowell about ignoring cognitive science. Do you find that germain to this discussion?

My working notion is that sentient practice is pragmatic natural corroboration in the service of norms of survival, flourishing, and sociality. Sapient practice is obviously supervenient on this version of sentient practice. This is clear from human infant development, the evolution of human language from non-linguistic human ancestors, quotidian experience with non-verbal behaviors like eating, driving, music, visual arts, walking, running, meditating.

I guess this puts me in an interdisciplinary arena roughly in cognitive science and developmental, evolutionary psychology. But perhaps with a broader interest in understanding in the context of 20th and 21st century intellectual history. Again, with an emphasis on philosophical rigour, but not sounding too parochially philosophical.

Can you find some adjacent frameworks in those disciplines?

You are not just borrowing from these fields—you are integrating them into a philosophical architecture:

Your synthesis:

Enactivism → organism–environment grounding

Predictive processing → formal model of corroboration

Developmental psychology → proto-theory before language

Ecological psychology → perceptual attunement

Pragmatism (Peirce/Lewis) → norm of truth beyond current justification

Quine → holism + revisability

Kant (naturalized) → constraints on possible experience

Your position allows you to say:

Normativity is neither imposed by language nor reducible to causation; it emerges in structured, corrigible interaction and is progressively articulated in discursive and institutional practices.

That is a strong alternative to:

Sellarsian inferentialism

Rortyan pragmatism

reductive naturalism

Since you want to reach:

philosophers

cognitive scientists

intellectual historians

you might frame your view in two layers:

Layer 1 (interdisciplinary, accessible)

“Cognition is a multi-level system of error-sensitive interaction with the environment, beginning before language and stabilizing in social practices.”

Layer 2 (philosophical precision)

“Truth is a norm governing these practices under the joint constraints of foundation, correspondence, and coherence.”

This lets each audience “hook in” at the right level.
