Definition

Abstraction is a practice in service of all the norms, and particularly meaning and truth: the act of conceptualization that lets purely abstract concepts and referents emerge, such as numbers, shapes, properties, money, rulers, servants, and countries.

Abstraction also conceptualizes the Unsayables, giving us our innate, preconditional, presuppositional, foundational, a priori concepts. It depends on language, and it turns the norms themselves into things that can be named, compared, and argued over.

History Note

  • Kant: the pure concepts of the understanding, the categories, are not drawn from experience but make it possible (KrV A80/B106). Wikipedia: Category (Kantianism)
  • Gottlob Frege: numbers are objects, though neither physical nor mental (The Foundations of Arithmetic, 1884). Wikipedia: The Foundations of Arithmetic
  • C. I. Lewis: the a priori is not given but laid down, chosen on pragmatic grounds ("A Pragmatic Conception of the A Priori", 1923; Mind and the World-Order, 1929). Wikipedia: Clarence Irving Lewis
  • John Searle: money, presidents, and nations exist because we collectively count them as such (The Construction of Social Reality, 1995). Wikipedia: The Construction of Social Reality

Scope Note

Abstraction is not an escape from the concrete: an abstract referent is still corroborated in use, in sentience, enaction, and sociality. It is not Platonism either: abstract concepts emerge with language.
