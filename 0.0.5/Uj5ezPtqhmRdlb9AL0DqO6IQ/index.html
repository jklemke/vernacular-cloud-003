Definition

Enaction is a practice in service of the norm of reality: intervening in the world and acting upon it, so that what happens next is partly the agent's doing. It is where DO separates from HAPPEN.

An agent that acts finds out, again and again, whether the world answers as expected. Each such round is a corroboration, in real time, of the mind-dependent notion of a mind-independent world (MDNMIW).

Example

A beaver dams a stream, and the valley becomes a pond. The world it lives in is partly one it has made.

History Note

  • Charles S. Peirce: belief is a habit of action, and inquiry the struggle to settle it ("The Fixation of Belief", 1877). Wikipedia: The Fixation of Belief
  • Ian Hacking: we believe in electrons because we can use them to intervene, not because we can represent them (Representing and Intervening, 1983). Wikipedia: Ian Hacking
  • Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson, and Eleanor Rosch: cognition as enaction, the bringing forth of a world (The Embodied Mind, 1991). Wikipedia: Enactivism
  • Anna Wierzbicka and Cliff Goddard's Natural Semantic Metalanguage treats DO and HAPPEN as separate semantic primes. Wikipedia: Natural semantic metalanguage

Scope Note

Enaction is not behaviour; it is not instinct nor is it reflex. It is not, here, a theory of cognition as in enactivism: the stress falls on acting upon the world to effect a result. Enaction can be carried out without language, and sociality begins when an enactive agent meets others like itself.
