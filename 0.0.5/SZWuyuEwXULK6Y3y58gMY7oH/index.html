Definition

Transcendence is a practice in service of the norm of legitimacy: the abstraction of reality and sociality into a generalized perspective, an abstract version of reality, truth, and meaning. It is the stance from which the Practical Realms are made legitimate.

Transcendence is intersubjective practice, grounded in the flourishing of the sentient and sapient creature within a society. It reaches toward the view from nowhere and the view from everywhere without ever leaving the creature that takes it.

History Note

  • Adam Smith: we judge our own conduct by imagining an impartial spectator (The Theory of Moral Sentiments, 1759, Part III). Wikipedia: The Theory of Moral Sentiments
  • William James: belief in the absolute allows us a "moral holiday" (Pragmatism, 1907, Lecture II). Wikipedia: William James
  • George Herbert Mead: the self takes the attitude of the "generalized other" (Mind, Self, and Society, 1934). Wikipedia: Generalized other
  • Thomas Nagel: objectivity as a view from nowhere, reached by stepping back from one's own perspective (The View from Nowhere, 1986). Wikipedia: The View from Nowhere

Scope Note

Transcendence is not the supernatural. Scientific materialism arises when, in trying to oubliate the LORD, one oubliates the supra-sentient by mistake; the cure is to drop the word "LORD" and keep the word transcendence. Nor is transcendence a moral holiday: the generalized stance is not a place of rest but the one from which practices are judged.
