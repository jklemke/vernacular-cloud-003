Definition

Energeia (ἐνέργεια) is the Greek word for being at work: activity, actuality, the realization of a dynamis. Aristotle seems to have coined it, from ergon, work, together with entelecheia, being at one's end.

The Latin schoolmen turned it into actus, act, and called God pure act. Physics turned it into energy, a conserved quantity with no activity in it at all.

Example

τὸ γὰρ ἔργον τέλος, ἡ δὲ ἐνέργεια τὸ ἔργον.

"For the work is the end, and the activity is the work." Aristotle, Metaphysics Θ.8, 1050a21

History Note

  • Aristotle sets energeia against dynamis (Metaphysics Θ) and defines the soul as the first entelecheia of a natural body that has life potentially (De Anima II.1, 412a27–28).
  • Latin renders energeia as actus and actualitas; Arabic as fiʿl, so that bi-l-fiʿl means "actually". Aquinas calls God actus purus, pure act, with no potentiality in it.
  • Gregory Palamas distinguishes God's unknowable essence (ousia) from his uncreated energies (energeiai), a distinction central to Orthodox theology (14th century). Wikipedia: Essence–energies distinction
  • Thomas Young gives "energy" its physical sense (Lectures on Natural Philosophy, 1807); 19th-century physics makes it the conserved quantity.

Scope Note

Energeia is not energy. Aristotle's word names a thing at work being what it is; the physicist's word names a quantity that is conserved through every change. In this nomenclature energy belongs with distance-duration-energy among the Categories, and Aristotle's actuality is closer to what the Practical Realms call doing as opposed to happening.
