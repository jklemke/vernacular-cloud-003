Definition

Aggregate is the ineffable notion of the many: group, collection, set, plurality, a motley plethora. Every attempt to say what an aggregate is either lists its members or states a rule for them, and both already presuppose isolates gathered together.

Like each of the Unsayables, aggregate cannot be defined; we must explicate it by use, in the prototype sentence: Subjectivity detects patterns of isolates, aggregates, and voids in time and space.

Example

A flock, a library, the even numbers, the people in this room: each is many, and each is one aggregate.

History Note

  • Cantor's theorem: the power set of any set is larger than the set itself, so there are more sets of sets than there are sets. Wikipedia: Cantor's theorem
  • Russell's paradox shows that not every rule forms a set (1901).
  • Mereology, from Leśniewski to Lewis's Parts of Classes (1991), treats wholes as fusions of parts. SEP: Mereology

Scope Note

Aggregates differ by how they are formed:

  • a fusion, by listing its elements (extensional)
  • a class, by a rule that specifies a property (intensional)
  • a set, by membership, where list and rule are interchangeable
  • a domain, by a metalanguage
