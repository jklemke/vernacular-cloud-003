Definition

Theōria (θεωρία) is the Greek word for viewing: first a delegation sent to watch a festival or consult an oracle, then the contemplation of what is eternal. Aristotle ranked the contemplative life highest, as the activity most like a god's; Plotinos made contemplation the source of all making; Christian writers took it as the vision of God.

Theory, theorem, and theatre all descend from the same seeing. Despite appearances, theōria is not built on theos, god.

Example

ὥστε ἡ τοῦ θεοῦ ἐνέργεια, μακαριότητι διαφέρουσα, θεωρητικὴ ἂν εἴη.

"So the activity of God, which surpasses all others in blessedness, would be contemplative." Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.8, 1178b21–22

History Note

  • A theōros was an envoy sent to a festival or an oracle; Herodotus has Solon travel abroad "for the sake of theōria", to see the world (1.29–30).
  • Plato's philosophers are "lovers of the spectacle of truth" (Republic V, 475e). Aristotle divides theoretical from practical and productive knowledge (Metaphysics E.1) and ranks the contemplative life highest (Nicomachean Ethics X.7–8).
  • Plotinos: all things, even nature, aim at contemplation, and making is a by-product of it (Enneads III.8). Christian writers from Evagrios to Pseudo-Dionysios make theōria the contemplation of God. Wikipedia: Theoria
  • Latin renders it contemplatio and speculatio, and sets the vita contemplativa against the vita activa; Arabic naẓar (speculation; al-ʿilm al-naẓarī, theoretical knowledge). Modern descendants: theory, theorem, theoretical; theatre comes from the same verb of seeing.

Scope Note

Theōria is not set apart from practice in this nomenclature. The generalized stance that Aristotle reserved for the contemplative life is transcendence, which is itself a practice, in service of legitimacy, grounded in the flourishing of a creature in society. There is no view from everywhere (Absolute Perspective); there is the practice of looking from further off.
