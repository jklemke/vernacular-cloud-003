Definition

Ambiguity is the monster of the labyrinth of reasons that makes one word lead in two directions: a term with multiple, possibly incompatible, meanings carries an argument from one meaning to another unnoticed. Among the modes of Agrippa it belongs to relativity: things appear differently as their relations, and our points of view, change.

Broader

  • labyrinth of reasons

Related

  • equivocation
  • vagueness

Notes

Example

Nothing is better than eternal happiness. A ham sandwich is better than nothing. Therefore a ham sandwich is better than eternal happiness.

Scope Note

Ambiguity is not only a hazard. Poetry lives by it, and so do the shifty, cluster notions of philosophy: knowledge, truth, existence. The labyrinth begins when an argument treats an ambiguous word as if it had a single meaning.

History Note

  • Aristotle: "being is said in many ways" (Metaph. Γ.2, 1003a33); equivocation heads his list of fallacies that depend on language (Soph. El. 4, 165b30 ff.).
  • Agrippa's third mode, relativity: an object appears so in relation to the judge and to what accompanies it, but what it is in its nature we cannot say (Sextos Empeirikos, PH I.167). perspective
  • William Empson's Seven Types of Ambiguity (1930) treats ambiguity as a resource of poetry rather than a defect.
  • Wittgenstein replaces the single essence with family resemblances (PI §§66–67) and calls philosophy a battle against the bewitchment of our intelligence by means of language (PI §109). SEP: Ambiguity
