Definition

A sentence is the unit of signification on which analytic philosophy, from Frege and Russell to Brandom, has focused: the smallest unit that can be asserted, denied, believed, or doubted. With the sentence come facts and false facts, lies and conjectures, and the sentence's role in conjecture, subterfuge, and fiction.

Sentences are formed by giving syntactic shape to the grammatical categories. The simplest form of a sentence is the concatenation of an indicate with a predicate.

Example

History Note

  • Gottlob Frege's context principle: never ask for the meaning of a word in isolation, but only in the context of a proposition (Die Grundlagen der Arithmetik, 1884, Introduction). Wikipedia: Context principle
  • Bertrand Russell analyses sentences about things that do not exist ("On Denoting", Mind, 1905). Wikipedia: On Denoting
  • Ludwig Wittgenstein: only the proposition has sense; only in the context of a proposition has a name meaning (Tractatus 3.3, 1921).
  • J. L. Austin: some sentences do things rather than describe them (How to Do Things with Words, 1962). Wikipedia: Performative utterance
  • Robert Brandom takes the sentence as the smallest unit that can make a move in the game of giving and asking for reasons (Making It Explicit, 1994).

Scope Note

The sentence is where the token illusion becomes the Myth of the Proven: the very idea of a proposition is that "snow is white" and snow is white have identical, symmetrical power.
