Definition

Interrogation is the grammatical operation of asking: who, what, where, when, why, how, whether. It opens a space that the other operations fill, and it may be what most clearly separates a fully grammatical language from the communication of other animals.

Notes

Example

Chimpanzees and gorillas taught to sign have answered questions, but they have rarely if ever been seen to ask one. Joseph Jordania asks what it took for the first question to be asked (Who Asked the First Question?, 2006).

History Note

  • Socrates practises the elenchus, the questioning that tests what others claim to know (Ap. 21b–23b).
  • R. G. Collingwood argues that a proposition can be understood only as the answer to a question (An Autobiography, 1939, ch. V).
  • Hans-Georg Gadamer asserts the hermeneutic priority of the question (Truth and Method, 1960, Part II).
  • Nuel Belnap and Thomas Steel develop a logic of questions and answers (The Logic of Questions and Answers, 1976).
  • Joseph Jordania traces the origins of the question, of intelligence and of speech, to choral singing (Who Asked the First Question?, 2006).

Scope Note

A question is not a statement with a gap. It sets the terms within which an answer can count as an answer, and so it carries presuppositions of its own. The Socratic question, the child's "Why?", and the scientist's hypothesis are all interrogation.
