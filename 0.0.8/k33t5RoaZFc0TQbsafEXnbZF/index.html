Definition

Psychē (ψυχή) is the Greek word for breath, life, and soul: what leaves the body at death. In Homer the psychē is a shade that flits off to Hades; for Plato it is the self that can know the Forms and outlive the body; for Aristotle it is the form of a living body, so that plants have a psychē too.

Psychē and pneuma both begin as breath, and Paul ranks the "psychic" person below the "pneumatic" one. English has never settled whether psychē means soul, mind, self, or life.

Example

ψυχή ἐστιν ἐντελέχεια ἡ πρώτη σώματος φυσικοῦ δυνάμει ζωὴν ἔχοντος.

"The soul is the first actuality of a natural body that has life potentially."

Aristotle, De Anima II.1, 412a27–28

History Note

  • In Homer the psychē leaves through the mouth at death and goes down to Hades, as Patroklos' and Hektor's do (Iliad 16.856, 22.362); in the underworld the dead are witless shades (Odyssey 11).
  • Plato argues for the soul's immortality (Phaedo), divides it into reason, spirit, and appetite (Republic IV, 435c–441c), and pictures it as a charioteer with two horses (Phaedrus 246a–254e).
  • Aristotle makes the soul the form of a living body, with nutritive, perceptive, and thinking powers (De Anima II.1–3); the Practical Realms begin from the same list. Wikipedia: On the Soul
  • Paul contrasts the psychikos anthrōpos, the merely natural person, with the pneumatikos, the spiritual one (1 Corinthians 2:14–15); the Gnostics make it a ranking (see hylē).
  • Latin renders it anima (life-soul) and animus (mind); Arabic nafs (soul, self), beside rūḥ (spirit), a pair that repeats psychē and pneuma. Ibn Sina's "flying man" argues that the soul knows itself without the body. Modern descendants: psychology, psychiatry, psychedelic, psyche.

Scope Note

Psychē is not the mind of modern philosophy, nor consciousness. Aristotle's psychē covers nutrition, sentience, and sapience at once; in this nomenclature these are separate practices, and the self that Plato's psychē was is the magisterium of IPSEITY. Whether psychē means mere sentience or sentience and sapience depends on whom one reads.
