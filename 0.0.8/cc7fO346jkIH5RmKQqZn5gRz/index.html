Definition

Grammar is the conceptual structure of a fully grammatical language: the operations of indication, predication, situation, stipulation, implication, composition, and interrogation. A fully grammatical language is what separates sapience from sentience. Bee dances, DNA, and the alarm calls of vervet monkeys are language-like, but none has all of these operations.

Aristotle called the human being the rational animal. Badinage: the human being is the animal with a fully grammatical language. One criterion for a true artificial general intelligence is that it would have to use language across all of these operational facets.

Example

Vervet monkeys give different alarm calls for leopards, eagles, and snakes, and their troop-mates respond to each in the appropriate way: up into the trees, down into the bushes, up on their hind legs to look around. The call indicates; it does not predicate, stipulate, or ask.

"Logic is the grammaticalization of sentient practice."

"Justice is the grammaticalization of social practice."

Victor Badinage

History Note

  • Aristotle: the human being alone among the animals has logos, speech and reason (Pol. I.2, 1253a9–10); the later formula is animal rationale.
  • Kant derives his categories from a table of the forms of judgment: quantity, quality, relation (categorical, hypothetical, disjunctive), and modality (KrV A70/B95).
  • Karl von Frisch decodes the waggle dance by which honeybees tell one another the direction and distance of food (The Dance Language and Orientation of Bees, 1965).
  • Charles Hockett lists the "design features" of human language, such as displacement, productivity, and duality of patterning, against animal communication ("The Origin of Speech", 1960). Wikipedia: Hockett's design features
  • Anna Wierzbicka and Cliff Goddard's Natural Semantic Metalanguage proposes some sixty-five semantic primes found in every language (Semantics: Primes and Universals, 1996). Wikipedia: Natural semantic metalanguage

Scope Note

Grammar is not syntax. Syntax is the engineering of strings; grammar is the set of operations by which a language indicates, predicates, situates, stipulates, implies, composes, and asks. The list is a suspension of token meanings, not a theory of linguistics, and it still has to be aligned with the semantic primes of Natural Semantic Metalanguage.
