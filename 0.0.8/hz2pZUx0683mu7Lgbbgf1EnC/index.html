Definition

An antinomy is a contradiction between two conclusions, both reached by correct reasoning. A conundrum originates in the improper use of logic; an antinomy originates in the proper use of logic. It arises when we take a generalization and turn it into a universality, and Badinage calls each such universality an Absolute.

Kant found four antinomies of pure reason. Badinage counts seven: Absolute Beginning, Absolute Description, Absolute Granularity, Absolute Justice, Absolute Necessity, Absolute Perspective, and Absolute Grammar. The recommendation is to label an antinomy as an antinomy rather than to argue about the details.

Example

Consider the set of all sets that are not members of themselves. If it is a member of itself, it is not; if it is not, it is.

"It is an error to take a generalization and turn it into a universality."

Victor Badinage

History Note

  • Zeno of Elea argues that motion is impossible because a runner must first cover half the distance, then half the remainder, without end (Aristotle, Phys. VI.9, 239b9–33). SEP: Zeno's Paradoxes
  • Kant sets out four antinomies of pure reason, each a thesis and an antithesis that reason proves with equal force: a beginning of the world, simple parts, freedom, and a necessary being (KrV A426/B454–A461/B489). Wikipedia: Kant's antinomies
  • Hegel answers that antinomies are not four but are found in every object, concept, and idea (Encyclopaedia Logic §48).
  • Russell finds an antinomy at the foundation of set theory, the set of all sets that are not members of themselves (letter to Frege, 1902).
  • Quine sorts paradoxes into the veridical, the falsidical, and antinomies, which "bring on the crises in thought" ("The Ways of Paradox", 1962).

Scope Note

An antinomy is not a monster of the labyrinth of reasons. The labyrinth leads us astray; an antinomy is where reasoning arrives when it has not gone astray. Kant "solved" the antinomies only in the sense that he identified them as antinomies, and that is the Badinagean solution too. Each Absolute is set out as Kant set out his: a Thesis and an Antithesis, both well argued.

The myths will not help us solve the antinomies; they may, however, help us cope with them.
