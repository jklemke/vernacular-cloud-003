Definition

Subjectivity is the ineffable notion of the first person perspective: the inescapable vantage point from which perception, cognition, affect, and self-modelling proceed. It is the condition under which objects appear, so it cannot itself appear as an object; every attempt to say what it is describes something else: soul, mind, brain, or self.

Like each of the Unsayables, subjectivity cannot be defined; we must explicate it by use, in the prototype sentence: Subjectivity detects patterns of isolates, aggregates, and voids in time and space.

Alternative Label

  • first-person perspective

Example

The same vantage point meets a bear in the woods (external), a toothache (internal), and a memory of last winter (central).

History Note

  • Hume looks within and finds only perceptions, never a self (Treatise 1.4.6); the Buddhist doctrine of anātman (no-self) makes the same discovery.
  • Kant: the "I think" must be able to accompany all my representations (KrV B131–132).
  • Russell: whether we are acquainted with our bare selves "is a very difficult one, upon which it would be rash to speak positively" (Problems of Philosophy, ch. 5).
  • The phenomenological tradition (Husserl, Heidegger's Dasein, Sartre, Merleau-Ponty) begins from the first person. SEP: Phenomenology

Scope Note

An umbrella term for the cluster of names for the first-person perspective (self, soul, spirit, ātman, mind, consciousness, apperception, Dasein), preferred here over consciousness. Discourse will not provide a model of subjectivity; subjectivity is the vantage point from which discourse proceeds.
