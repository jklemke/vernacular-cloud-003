Definition

The magistrator is the Name of God as the judge: the divine tribunal, the Last Judgment, the rule by which all are finally measured.

Is there a magistrator? No, but there is judgment: the question of MORALITY, "By what rule?", answered case by case in human tribunals, the work of jurisprudence. The divine command is the oubliation of justice: justice deferred to the mercy of the apocalypse buys purpose in the here and now.

History Note

  • Plato's myth of the judgment of souls after death (Gorgias 523a–527a).
  • The sheep and the goats: the Son of Man judges the nations (Matthew 25:31–46).
  • Kant: God as a postulate of practical reason, so that happiness may match virtue (Critique of Practical Reason 5:124–132, 1788).
  • William James: belief in God licenses a "moral holiday" (Pragmatism, Lecture II, 1907).

Scope Note

The magistrator judges from the Absolute Perspective. MORALITY asks for the general perspective instead, and human tribunals, however fallible, are where justice is done on earth (see Absolute Justice).
