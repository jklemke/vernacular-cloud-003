Definition

Molecularity is an interpretive framework of parts and wholes: it explores the mind-independent world as isolates composed into aggregates, from quarks to atoms, molecules, and DNA. It is a method as much as a realm: understand a thing by the parts it is made of.

Volcanoes, earthworms, and crows are all made of molecules. Molecularity is good for chemical elements and bad for abstract notions.

Example

Dmitri Mendeleev left gaps in his periodic table (1869) for elements no one had seen. Gallium (1875), scandium (1879), and germanium (1886) filled them, with the properties he had predicted.

History Note

  • Leucippus and Democritus: the world is atoms and the void. Wikipedia: Atomism
  • Plutarch's Ship of Theseus, whose planks are all replaced (Life of Theseus 23); Nāgasena's chariot, which is none of its parts (Milindapañha). Wikipedia: Ship of Theseus
  • John Dalton's atomic theory (A New System of Chemical Philosophy, 1808). Wikipedia: John Dalton
  • Dmitri Mendeleev's periodic table (1869). Wikipedia: Periodic table
  • Peter Unger, "There Are No Ordinary Things" (1979): mereological nihilism, only simples exist. Wikipedia: Mereological nihilism

Scope Note

Molecularity is not the discipline of chemistry: particle physics, chemistry, and biochemistry all work in it. Nor is it materialism, and it does not solve the first person problem. Its labyrinth is the puzzle of what a whole is (the Ship of Theseus, the chariot, mereological nihilism, substance), and the puzzles of quantum physics are best thought of as puzzles of molecularity. The whole is often the cause: the sun moves its atoms.
