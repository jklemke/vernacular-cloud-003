Definition

Epistēmē (ἐπιστήμη) is the Greek word for knowledge, especially knowledge that can give an account of itself, and for a science, a body of such knowledge. Plato set it against doxa, opinion; Aristotle made it knowledge through explanations (aitiai), demonstrated from first principles.

Plato's Theaetetus asks what epistēmē is, tries three answers, and ends without one. The question has not been settled since.

History Note

  • Plato's Theaetetus tests knowledge as perception, as true opinion, and as true opinion with an account (logos), refutes all three, and ends in aporia (210a–b). Wikipedia: Theaetetus (dialogue)
  • Aristotle: we have epistēmē of a thing when we know its explanation (aitia), and that it could not be otherwise (Posterior Analytics I.2, 71b9–12). Epistēmē is one of five intellectual virtues, with technē, phronēsis, sophia, and nous (Nicomachean Ethics VI.3, 1139b15–17).
  • Latin renders it scientia, whence science; Arabic ʿilm. James Frederick Ferrier coins "epistemology" (Institutes of Metaphysic, 1854). Wikipedia: Epistemology
  • Michel Foucault uses épistémè for the unspoken order of knowledge in an age (Les Mots et les choses, 1966).

Scope Note

Epistēmē is not one thing that a definition could capture: knowledge is a cluster notion, and the attempt to define it, from the Theaetetus to the Gettier problem, is the Socratic Miscarriage. In this nomenclature what we call knowledge rests on corroboration, and Theaetetus' first answer is not as wrong as Socrates made it look: sentience is where corroboration starts.
