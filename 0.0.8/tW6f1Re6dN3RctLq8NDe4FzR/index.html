Definition

Subterfuge is the monster of the labyrinth of reasons that deceives us: the lie, the sophism, the prevarication, and the persiflage that turns a question aside with a joke. The other monsters lead us astray by the nature of reasoning; subterfuge leads us astray on purpose. It is one of the two monsters Badinage adds to the classical tropes.

Broader

  • labyrinth of reasons

Notes

Example

Plato's Socrates proposes a "noble lie" for the guardians of his city: a myth of metals, in which the god mixed gold into the rulers, silver into the auxiliaries, and iron and bronze into the farmers and craftsmen (Rep. 414b–415d).

The Second Letter of Peter, which claims Simon Peter as its author and was very probably written by someone else, assures its readers that it does not follow "cleverly devised myths" (2 Pet 1:16).

Scope Note

Subterfuge is intentional. ambiguity and disputation can lead us astray in good faith; subterfuge exploits them.

History Note

  • Plato's Sophist hunts the sophist as a maker of false images and a seller of the appearance of wisdom (Soph. 231d–e, 268c–d). Wikipedia: Sophist (dialogue)
  • Aristotle's Sophistical Refutations catalogues thirteen fallacies, six that depend on language and seven that do not (Soph. El. 4–5).
  • Schopenhauer's The Art of Being Right (Eristische Dialektik, c. 1831) gives thirty-eight stratagems for winning an argument whether one is right or not.
  • Harry Frankfurt distinguishes the bullshitter, who does not care what is true, from the liar, who must keep track of it in order to hide it (On Bullshit, 1986).
