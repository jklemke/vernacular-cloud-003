Definition

Implication is the grammatical operation of drawing one thing from another: if this, then that. Inference, deduction, contingency, justification. The subjunctive, the conditional, the counterfactual. The space of reasons, the realm of what-could-have-otherwise and what-is-yet-to-come.

Notes

Example

In Lewis Carroll's dialogue, the Tortoise accepts that A and B are true, and that if A and B then Z, but will not accept Z until that rule is written down as a further premise, and then another, without end ("What the Tortoise Said to Achilles", 1895).

History Note

  • Aristotle defines a deduction as speech in which, certain things being laid down, something other than them follows of necessity from their being so (An. Pr. I.1, 24b18–20).
  • The Stoics, from Philo to Chrysippus, dispute the truth conditions of the conditional (Sextos Empeirikos, M. VIII.112–117).
  • C. I. Lewis proposes "strict implication" against the paradoxes of material implication (1912; A Survey of Symbolic Logic, 1918).
  • Wilfrid Sellars argues that material rules of inference are as essential to meaning as formal ones ("Inference and Meaning", 1953); Robert Brandom builds on this (Making It Explicit, 1994). SEP: Wilfrid Sellars

Scope Note

The articulation of contingency and compatibility, not causality, is what logic does with implication. Formal implication follows the shape of the sentence; material inference, as Sellars and Brandom argue, follows the content of the concepts ("It's raining, so the streets will be wet"). Carroll's Tortoise shows that implication cannot be reduced to one more premise: it is an operation, not a proposition, which is why it belongs to grammar.
