Definition

Proliferation is the monster of the labyrinth of reasons that never lets us reach the end: every reason needs a further reason, every sign another sign to interpret it, and every answer raises more questions than it settles. It is a never-ending search for unmoved movers, unjustified justifiers, and unexplained explainers.

It is the infinite regress of the Münchhausen trilemma and the second mode of Agrippa.

Notes

Example

A child asks "Why?" and is given a reason, then asks "Why?" of the reason, and again of the next one. The child is a born philosopher, and so is the parent who finally says, "Because I said so."

"Infinite semiosis is just one of the many things that people don't like about philosophy."

Victor Badinage

Scope Note

Badinage prefers "proliferation" to "regress". A regress runs back along one line; proliferation also branches outward, in a combinatorial explosion, as each answer opens more questions. The parent's "Because I said so" is not a solution but an exit by another path: prejudice.

History Note

  • Aristotle argues that if everything required demonstration there would be an infinite regress, so some knowledge must be indemonstrable (An. Post. I.3, 72b5–24). SEP: Infinite Regress Arguments
  • Agrippa's second mode, regress ad infinitum: whatever is offered as proof needs a further proof (Sextos Empeirikos, PH I.166).
  • Peirce makes every sign interpretable only by a further sign, its interpretant, so that semiosis has no natural end (CP 2.303); Umberto Eco calls this "unlimited semiosis". SEP: Peirce's Theory of Signs
  • Hans Albert's Münchhausen trilemma (1968) makes the infinite regress one of the three ends of every attempt at ultimate justification.
