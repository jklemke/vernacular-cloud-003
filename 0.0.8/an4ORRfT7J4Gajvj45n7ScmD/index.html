Definition

Ēthos (ἦθος) is the Greek word for character: the settled disposition of a person, and the customary ways of a people. Its earliest sense is a haunt, the accustomed place of animals.

Aristotle traced it to ethos (ἔθος), habit: character is what habit makes. Cicero coined moralis to translate it, so ethics and morality are one word in two languages.

Example

ἡ δ' ἠθικὴ ἐξ ἔθους περιγίνεται, ὅθεν καὶ τοὔνομα ἔσχηκε μικρὸν παρεκκλῖνον ἀπὸ τοῦ ἔθους.

"Ethical virtue comes about from habit, which is also how it got its name, a slight variation on ethos."

Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.1, 1103a17–18

History Note

  • In Homer, ēthea are the haunts and pastures of horses (Iliad 6.511).
  • Herakleitos: ἦθος ἀνθρώπῳ δαίμων, "character is a man's fate" (fr. B119).
  • Aristotle derives ēthos from ethos, habit (Nicomachean Ethics II.1, 1103a17–18), and makes the speaker's character one of three means of persuasion, with pathos and logos (Rhetoric I.2, 1356a).
  • Cicero coins moralis from mores to translate ēthikos (De Fato 1). Arabic akhlāq (dispositions, character) names the science of ethics (Miskawayh, Tahdhīb al-akhlāq, 11th century). Wikipedia: Ethos
  • Modern descendants: ethics, ethos, ethology. Hegel's Sittlichkeit, from Sitte (custom), repeats the Greek move from custom to character to ethics.

Scope Note

Ēthos is not a theory of right action. In this nomenclature what ēthos named is spread across the Norms (above all flourishing), sociality with its loyalty, empathy, and fairness, and the magisterium of MORALITY. Modern philosophy has made ethics and morality into two theories; the words were one.
