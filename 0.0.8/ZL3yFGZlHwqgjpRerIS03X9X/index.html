Definition

Ousia (οὐσία) is the Greek word for substance or essence, formed from the participle of "to be". In everyday Greek it meant property: one's estate, one's substance in the sense of wealth.

Plato used it for the being of the Forms; Aristotle for the primary things, this man, this horse, and also for what a thing essentially is. At Nicaea in 325 it became the word on which Christian orthodoxy turned.

History Note

  • In the parable of the prodigal son, the younger son asks for his share of the ousia and squanders it (Luke 15:12–13): the word's everyday sense, property.
  • Aristotle's primary ousia is the individual, "this man" or "this horse" (Categories 5, 2a11–14); in Metaphysics Z, ousia becomes the essence, "what it was to be" a thing. The two senses pull against each other from the start. Wikipedia: Ousia
  • The Council of Nicaea (325) declares the Son homoousios, of one ousia with the Father; the Cappadocian Fathers then distinguish one ousia from three hypostaseis. Wikipedia: Homoousion
  • Latin renders it substantia and essentia (Augustine prefers essentia, De Trinitate V.2.3); Arabic jawhar (substance) and dhāt (essence, self).

Scope Note

Ousia names two things that cannot both be primary: the particular and the essence. In this nomenclature what a thing is taken to be depends on how it is indicated: as an isolate or an aggregate, or as a quiddity. The Nicene use shows how far a philosophical word can travel from its first meaning, property, and still carry authority.
