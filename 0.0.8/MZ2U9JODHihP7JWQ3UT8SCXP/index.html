Definition

Sophia (σοφία) is the Greek word for wisdom, and first for skill: the cleverness of a carpenter or a poet. Philosophy is the love of it; the sophist claimed to have it; Socrates claimed only to know that he did not.

In Jewish and Christian scripture Wisdom is personified, with God at the creation, and Paul calls Christ the wisdom of God. Latin rendered it sapientia, from which this nomenclature takes sapience.

History Note

  • In Homer, sophiē is a craftsman's skill, the shipwright's (Iliad 15.411–412). Pythagoras is said to have coined philosophia, calling himself not wise but a lover of wisdom (Diogenes Laertios I.12; Cicero, Tusculan Disputations V.3.8–9).
  • Socrates' wisdom is to know that he does not know (Apology 20d–23b). For Aristotle sophia is the highest knowledge, of first principles, combining nous and epistēmē (Metaphysics A.1–2; Nicomachean Ethics VI.7).
  • In the Septuagint, Wisdom speaks of being with God before creation (Proverbs 8:22–31); the Wisdom of Solomon follows. Paul: Christ "the power of God and the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24, NRSVue). Justinian's Hagia Sophia (537) is dedicated to Holy Wisdom. In Gnostic myth Sophia's fall begins the material world (Apocryphon of John). Wikipedia: Sophia (wisdom)
  • Latin renders it sapientia; Arabic ḥikma, which also became a name for philosophy itself. Modern descendants: philosophy, sophist, sophistry, sophisticated, sophomore.

Scope Note

Sophia is not knowledge, and it is not cleverness, though it once meant both. In this nomenclature sapience keeps the Latin word but means a practice, the use of ostensive language in service of truth and meaning, not a possession. Socrates' ignorance is the honest form of the Socratic Miscarriage: the discovery that the definitions were not to be had.
