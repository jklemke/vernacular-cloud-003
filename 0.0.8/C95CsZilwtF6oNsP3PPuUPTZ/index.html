Definition

Ostension is the mode of signification by which social creatures with a fully grammatical language refer to actual and possible situations in their shared physical and social environment. It is the downtown of language: the grammaticalization of sentient practice and social practice, from which metaphorical, abstract, mythical, and transcendent language emerges.

Ostensive language is not fact-stating; it is possibility-stating. It indexes a position in a corroborative scenario by situating aspects of objects in it. The stipulation is implicit in the pragmatics, and natural language has words that make it explicit: says, believes, knows.

Notes

Example

A child sees a dog, points, and says dog. The parent answers, Yes, a dog. And there's another one. A position in a shared scenario has been indexed, and a possibility has been opened.

History Note

  • Augustine recalls learning words as his elders named things and turned toward them (Conf. I.8.13); Wittgenstein opens the Philosophical Investigations with this passage (1953, §1).
  • Wittgenstein: an ostensive definition can be variously interpreted in every case (PI §28).
  • W. V. O. Quine's "gavagai": pointing cannot settle whether the speaker means a rabbit, a rabbit stage, or an undetached rabbit part (Word and Object, 1960, ch. 2). Wikipedia: Indeterminacy of translation
  • J. L. Austin mocks the philosophers' diet of "moderate-sized specimens of dry goods" (Sense and Sensibilia, 1962).
  • Michael Tomasello: pointing and pantomime are the evolutionary foundation of human communication (Origins of Human Communication, 2008).

Scope Note

Metalanguage, metaphysics, and suspensional language attempt to articulate the preconditions of ostensive language. Transcendent language attempts to articulate intersubjective concepts as if they were ostensive.
