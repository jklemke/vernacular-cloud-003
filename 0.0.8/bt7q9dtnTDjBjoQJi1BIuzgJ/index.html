Definition

Liberty, governance, and revolt are a conceptual device of MORALITY: liberty is obedience to a rule one gives oneself; governance is a rule imposed by others; revolt is the refusal of a rule that has lost its legitimacy.

Governance earns obedience while it is legitimate. Revolt, whether uprising, revolution, or mutiny, is the remedy when it is not, and becomes governance in its turn.

Example

Freedom springs from following a self-imposed rule. The closest we get to free will is in our attempt to follow an articulated goal. Kant folllowing Jean-Jacques Rousseau’s The Social Contract: “Obedience to a law which we prescribe to ourselves is freedom.” (Original French: “L’obéissance à la loi qu’on s’est prescrite est liberté.”)

Victor Badinage

History Note

  • John Locke: a people may resist a government that breaks its trust (Second Treatise of Government §§222–225, 1689).
  • Rousseau: obedience to a law one prescribes to oneself is liberty (The Social Contract I.8, 1762).
  • Kant: autonomy, the will's giving the law to itself (Groundwork 4:440, 1785). SEP: Kant's Moral Philosophy
  • Hannah Arendt distinguishes revolution, the founding of freedom, from mere rebellion (On Revolution, 1963).

Scope Note

The device is not a political theory. It names the moral life of a rule: given to oneself, imposed by others, refused. The norm that decides between governance and revolt is legitimacy; the antinomy nearby is Absolute Justice.
