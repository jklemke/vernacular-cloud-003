Definition

Doxa (δόξα) is the Greek word for opinion, belief, what seems to be so, and also for reputation, how one seems to others. Parmenides set it against truth, Plato against knowledge (epistēmē).

In the Septuagint and the New Testament the same word came to mean glory, the radiance of God. A word for mere seeming became a word for the highest worth.

History Note

  • Parmenides' goddess teaches both the heart of truth and "the opinions of mortals, in which there is no true trust" (fr. B1.28–30).
  • Plato places doxa between knowledge and ignorance (Republic V, 476d–480a); the Theaetetus tries knowledge as true doxa, then as true doxa with a logos, and ends without an answer (187b–210b).
  • The Septuagint renders the Hebrew kavod (weight, honour, glory) as doxa; the Latin Bible has gloria, the philosophers opinio. Arabic ẓann (supposition) and raʾy (view) carry the philosophical sense.
  • Modern descendants: orthodoxy ("right doxa"), heterodoxy, paradox, doxology. Husserl revives doxa for belief, Bourdieu for what a society takes for granted. Wikipedia: Doxa

Scope Note

Doxa is not simply error. In this nomenclature most of what anyone knows rests on corroboration, and the Labyrinth of Reasons, from prejudice to disputation, is where opinion lives. That the same word means opinion and glory is a clue: what seems so to others is what one is worth to them.
