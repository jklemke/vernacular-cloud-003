Definition

Time is the ineffable notion of before, now, and after. We order events by it, measure it, and live within it, yet every attempt to define it either presupposes it or swaps it for something else: motion, number, feeling, or coordinate.

Like each of the Unsayables, time cannot be defined; we must explicate it by use, in the prototype sentence: Subjectivity detects patterns of isolates, aggregates, and voids in time and space.

Notes

Example

"What then is time? If no one asks me, I know; if I wish to explain it to one who asks, I know not."

Augustine, Conf. XI.14.17

The word "now" is true every time it is said, and never of the same moment twice.

History Note

  • Aristotle calls time "the number of motion in respect of before and after" (Phys. IV.11, 219b1–2).
  • Augustine places time in the mind as a threefold present: memory, attention, and expectation (Conf. XI.20.26).
  • Kant makes time the a priori form of inner sense (KrV A33/B49–50). SEP: Kant's Views on Space and Time
  • McTaggart's A-series (past, present, future) and B-series (earlier, later) frame the modern debate; he argued that time is unreal. Wikipedia: The Unreality of Time
  • Heidegger makes temporality (Zeitlichkeit) the horizon of Dasein's being (Being and Time, 1927).

Scope Note

The nomenclature takes no side in the metaphysics of time: tensed or tenseless, flowing or block, absolute or relational. It names what those theories share and cannot do without.
