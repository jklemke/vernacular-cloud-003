Definition

Morphē (μορφή) is the Greek word for shape: the visible form of a body, and often its beauty. Aristotle used it beside hylē: every natural thing is shaped matter, and morphē often stands in for eidos.

In the hymn of Philippians, Christ "existed in the form of God" and took "the form of a slave". Latin forma and Arabic ṣūra render both morphē and eidos, so that two Greek words became one.

Example

"Who, though he existed in the form of God, ... emptied himself, taking the form of a slave."

Philippians 2:6–7 (NRSVue)

History Note

  • Aristotle: "by matter I mean the bronze, by shape (morphē) the figure of its form, and by what is made of them the statue" (Metaphysics Z.3, 1029a3–5).
  • Philippians 2:6–7 sets morphē theou against morphē doulou; Christ's self-emptying (kenōsis) becomes a theme of Christology. At the transfiguration Jesus metemorphōthē, was transformed (Mark 9:2).
  • Latin renders both morphē and eidos as forma; Arabic both as ṣūra. Wikipedia: Hylomorphism
  • Ovid's Latin Metamorphoses keeps a Greek title; his Morpheus, the god who shapes dreams, gives morphine its name (Friedrich Sertürner, 1817). Goethe coins Morphologie for the study of living form (1790s).
  • Modern descendants: morphology, metamorphosis, amorphous, isomorphic, -morphic.

Scope Note

Morphē overlaps eidos, and the overlap is the point: the visible shape and the intelligible kind were never cleanly separated, and the translators merged them. In this nomenclature shape belongs to the Physical Realms (molecularity, cohesion) and to pattern; grammomorphic, Badinage's provisional coinage, borrows the word for what takes the shape of grammar.
