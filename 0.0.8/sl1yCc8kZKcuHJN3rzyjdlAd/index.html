Definition

INFINITY is the contested space of questions like "Where does it end?" The infinitely large and the infinitely small, the countable and the uncountable, the continuum, the calculation that never halts. Its conceptual devices are initial-successor-index and selectability-divisibility-composability.

First-cause and prime-mover arguments misapply INFINITY and ENTIRETY: they treat an endless series as a bounded whole, then ask what stands at its edge.

Example

A hotel with infinitely many rooms, all of them full, can still take in a new guest: each guest moves up one room. (Hilbert's hotel)

History Note

  • Aristotle allows the potential infinite and denies the actual (Physics III.6, 206a9–b27); Zeno's paradoxes (Physics VI.9).
  • Aquinas's first two ways argue to a first mover and a first cause from the impossibility of an infinite regress (Summa Theologiae I q.2 a.3).
  • Georg Cantor proves that the real numbers cannot be counted (1874, 1891); Hilbert: no one shall drive us from the paradise Cantor has created ("Über das Unendliche", 1926). Wikipedia: Cantor's diagonal argument
  • Alan Turing shows that no general procedure can decide whether a calculation halts (1936). Wikipedia: Halting problem

Scope Note

INFINITY is not a number, and not the ENTIRETY: the entirety is bounded, while the infinite is what no boundary contains. Infinite search time raises its own question: could a Boltzmann brain be located, and if not, does it exist? The antinomy at home in INFINITY is Absolute Granularity.
