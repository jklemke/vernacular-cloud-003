Definition

Complete, enumerated, and bounded are a conceptual device of ENTIRETY: three ways of claiming a totality. A totality is complete when nothing is left out, enumerated when every member can be listed, and bounded when an edge can be drawn around it. Any one can hold without the others.

Example

The whole numbers can be listed, but there is no last one. The points between 0 and 1 have an edge on each side, but cannot be listed.

History Note

  • Kant's category of limitation, the third of Quality (KrV A80/B106).
  • John Venn's diagrams draw a bounded universe of discourse (1880). Wikipedia: Venn diagram
  • Georg Cantor proves that the real numbers cannot be enumerated (1874). Wikipedia: Cantor's first set theory article
  • Kurt Gödel: no consistent formal system rich enough for arithmetic is complete (1931). Wikipedia: Gödel's incompleteness theorems

Scope Note

The entirety is bounded; the apeiron lies outside every boundary. A Venn diagram draws the edge and oubliates what lies beyond it. The device echoes Kant's limitation, but its terms come from logic and set theory.
