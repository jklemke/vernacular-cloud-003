Definition

A practical realm is a practice in service of a norm: what living things, and eventually persons, do, as opposed to what merely happens to them. The seven Practical Realms are nutrition, sentience, enaction, sociality, sapience, abstraction, and transcendence.

Practices and norms are many-to-many: nutrition serves survival, and every other practice presupposes it. The first four can be carried out without language; the last three depend on it, and so on the Semantics. Each practice emerges from the Physical Realms and the Unsayables beneath it, but under no neat, deterministic formula.

History Note

  • Aristotle distinguishes the powers of soul as nutritive, appetitive, perceptive, locomotive, and thinking; plants have only the first, and only some animals have all (De Anima II.3, 414a29–b19). Wikipedia: On the Soul
  • Charles S. Peirce's pragmatic maxim: our idea of a thing is our idea of its practical effects; belief is a habit of action ("How to Make Our Ideas Clear", 1878). Wikipedia: Pragmatic maxim
  • Charles W. Morris divides the study of signs into syntax, semantics, and pragmatics (Foundations of the Theory of Signs, 1938). Wikipedia: Pragmatics
  • Cheryl Misak retells pragmatism's history with Peirce and C. I. Lewis at its centre, apart from James and Rorty (The American Pragmatists, 2013). Wikipedia: Cheryl Misak

Scope Note

The Practical Realms are not Aristotle's souls, nor a theory of action. Nor are they the norms: survival, flourishing, reality, beauty, meaning, truth, and legitimacy are what the practices answer to. The line between the four practices that can do without language and the three that cannot is drawn lightly: language is itself a practice that grew out of the others.
