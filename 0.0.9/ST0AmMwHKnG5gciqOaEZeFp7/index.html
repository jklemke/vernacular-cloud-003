Definition

The Myth of the Nomen is the spell that falls on ostensive practice when the objects of the WORLD are taken to be name-shaped, sentence-shaped objects. Nominalism, the philosophical position that only particulars exist and universals are nothing but names, is its most deliberate form: it accepts the world of named things and denies whatever no name picks out. A proper name is not a description; a proper name is an arbitrarily nominated indication of a situation.

Notes

Example

"And whatsoever Adam called every living creature, that was the name thereof."

Genesis 2:19 (KJV)

History Note

  • Plato's Cratylus asks whether names belong to things by nature or by convention.
  • William of Ockham holds that universals are names or concepts, not things, and that entities are not to be multiplied beyond necessity (Summa Logicae I, c. 1323). SEP: Nominalism in Metaphysics
  • John Stuart Mill holds that proper names denote without connoting (A System of Logic, 1843, I.2.5).
  • Bertrand Russell analyzes names as disguised definite descriptions ("On Denoting", 1905). SEP: Descriptions
  • Saul Kripke answers that proper names are rigid designators, fixed by an initial baptism (Naming and Necessity, 1980).

Scope Note

A name may indicate a supra-sentient situation, a supra-sapient description of an actual situation, or an exapted (metaphorical, emergent) situation. In this sense "situation" emerges as our elusive word par excellence. Russell's theory of definite descriptions is a language for conjecture: acquaintance is recognition, which supports communal naming; definite description strays into jurisdictional naming. Ostensive practice is not discredited by calling it the Myth of the Nomen.
