Definition

Disputation is the monster of the labyrinth of reasons that feeds the contrarian in each of us: for every argument there is an opposing argument of equal force, and the dispute never closes. It is the first mode of Agrippa, the mode from dissent.

Broader

  • labyrinth of reasons

Notes

Example

In 155 BCE the Academic skeptic Karneades came to Rome as an ambassador from Athens. One day he spoke in praise of justice; the next day he spoke, just as persuasively, against it. Cato the Elder urged the Senate to send him home before he corrupted the young.

History Note

  • Protagoras holds that on every matter there are two arguments opposed to each other (Diogenes Laertios, Lives IX.51).
  • Pyrrhonian skepticism makes the equal force (isostheneia) of opposed arguments the road to suspension of judgment (epochē) (Sextos Empeirikos, PH I.8–10). SEP: Ancient Skepticism
  • Agrippa's first mode, dissent (diaphōnia): undecidable disagreement among philosophers and ordinary people alike (PH I.165).
  • The medieval disputatio, and Abelard's Sic et Non, set authority against authority as a method of teaching.
  • Kant's antinomies: pure reason proves both a thesis and its antithesis (KrV A426/B454 ff.).

Scope Note

Disputation is not subterfuge: both sides may argue in good faith. When the aim is to win rather than to find out, as in eristic and sophistry, disputation has become subterfuge.
