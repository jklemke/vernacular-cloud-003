Definition

Nous (νοῦς) is the Greek word for mind and intellect, and for the sense of a thing: what one "has in mind". Anaxagoras made nous the mind that set the cosmos in order; Aristotle made it the power that grasps first principles and, at its highest, the unmoved mover thinking itself; Plotinos made it the second hypostasis, flowing from the One.

Through the Neoplatonic synthesis nous became the divine mind of Christian, Jewish, and Islamic theology: God as Intellect.

History Note

  • In Homer, noos is the understanding of a person, or a plan: Odysseus saw the cities of many men "and learned their mind" (Odyssey 1.3).
  • Anaxagoras: "mind set all things in order" (πάντα διεκόσμησε νοῦς, fr. B12). Plato's Socrates is disappointed that Anaxagoras then explains nothing by it (Phaedo 97b–99d).
  • Aristotle: nous grasps the first principles from which demonstration starts (Posterior Analytics II.19; Nicomachean Ethics VI.6); the active intellect "makes all things" (De Anima III.5, 430a10–25); the unmoved mover is thought thinking itself (Metaphysics Λ.9).
  • Plotinos makes Nous the second hypostasis, after the One and before Soul (Enneads V.1). Wikipedia: Nous
  • Latin renders it intellectus and mens; Arabic ʿaql. al-Farabi, Ibn Sina, and Ibn Rushd build theories of the Active Intellect (al-ʿaql al-faʿʿāl); Ibn Rushd's claim that there is one intellect for all humans divides the Latin schools. Modern descendants: noetic, noumenon, noosphere.

Scope Note

Nous is not a faculty in this nomenclature. What nous named is the practice of sapience and abstraction, and the self that does the thinking is IPSEITY. God as Intellect, the Neoplatonic nous become divine, is a theme of the Socratic-Abrahamic civilization, and "artificial intelligence" inherits the picture.
