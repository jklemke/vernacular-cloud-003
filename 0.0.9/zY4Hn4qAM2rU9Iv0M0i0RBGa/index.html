Definition

The Myth of the Scriven is the spell that falls on cultural practice when the written word is taken for the bearer of truth: holy scripture, the Western Canon, the Great Books, the published record. Each new way of writing renews the spell: the invention of writing, of printing and publishing, of social media, of large language models.

Notes

Example

"This discovery of yours will create forgetfulness in the learners' souls, because they will not use their memories."

The Egyptian king Thamus to Theuth, the inventor of writing, in Plato, Phaedrus 275a (trans. Jowett)

Plato wrote the warning down, with all his literary mastery.

History Note

  • Writing is invented in Mesopotamia, in cuneiform, around 3200 BCE. Wikipedia: History of writing
  • Plato's Socrates warns that writing gives the appearance of wisdom without the reality (Phaedr. 274c–277a); the author of the Seventh Letter says there is no writing of his on the things that matter most (341c).
  • Gutenberg's movable type (c. 1450) makes the printed book, and the printed scripture, cheap and identical.
  • The Great Books of the Western World (1952) and Harold Bloom's The Western Canon (1994) defend a canon.
  • "Publish or perish" names the academic rule that the record of research becomes its measure (Logan Wilson, The Academic Man, 1942).

Scope Note

Writing preserves, and the scriven is not to be despised: this nomenclature is itself written. The spell falls when the text is taken for the truth it records: when a constitution becomes holy writ, when the canon replaces the inquiry, when publish or perish makes the record the goal. Cultural practice is not discredited by calling it the Myth of the Scriven.
