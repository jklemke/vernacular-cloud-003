Definition

LANGUAGE is the contested space of questions like "What can be said?" It is logos broadly conceived: the grammatical, syntactic, and semantic operations that enable sapient practice, the grammaticalization of sentient and social practice. Its conceptual devices are name-description-explanation and word-sentence-discourse.

A natural language contains its own metavocabulary. That is what distinguishes a fully grammatical language from a synthetic one, or from an informational code like DNA: a grammatical strange loop, comparable to Hofstadter's strange loop of consciousness. Philosophy is the metalanguage that must form a part of any natural language.

Example

It is not that language is completely incapable of articulating its foundation. It is that language can only achieve such an articulation through the incorporation of a regimented meta-language. The passage from vocalization to language is only complete after it has articulated such a metalanguage.

Victor Badinage

History Note

  • Alfred Tarski separates an object language from the metalanguage in which its truth is defined ("The Concept of Truth in Formalized Languages", 1933). SEP: Tarski's Truth Definitions
  • Rudolf Carnap divides the study of language into pragmatics, semantics, and syntax by what is abstracted away: the user, then the designata (Introduction to Semantics, 1942, §4).
  • Douglas Hofstadter: the self as a strange loop (Gödel, Escher, Bach, 1979; I Am a Strange Loop, 2007). Wikipedia: Strange loop
  • Robert Brandom: an autonomous discursive practice is one that could be engaged in though no other were (Between Saying and Doing, 2008). Wikipedia: Robert Brandom

Scope Note

LANGUAGE is not the Grammar or the Semantics, which are its tools, nor its Greek forerunner logos. Language can talk about things (ostension), about talking (metalanguage), and about talkers (heterosubjectivity). The antinomy at home in LANGUAGE is Absolute Grammar; see also grammomorphic.
