Definition

Commerce is the jurisdiction of exchange at its most general: trade, property, value, debt, and reciprocity.

Its tribunal is the market, and the price is its verdict; but debt and reciprocity are older than markets and money. Money, interest, investment, insurance, and charity are among its institutions.

History Note

  • Aristotle distinguishes household management from the art of getting wealth for its own sake (Politics I.8–10).
  • John Locke: property arises from mixing one's labour with what nature provides (Second Treatise of Government ch. 5, 1689).
  • Adam Smith: the propensity to truck, barter, and exchange (The Wealth of Nations I.2, 1776).
  • Marcel Mauss: the obligation to give, to receive, and to reciprocate (The Gift, 1925). Wikipedia: The Gift (book)
  • David Graeber: debt comes before money and markets (Debt: The First 5,000 Years, 2011).

Scope Note

Commerce is not economics, the discipline that studies it. Insurance and charity are public goods as well as trades.
