Definition

Composition is the grammatical operation of combining: AND, OR, and NOT join words into clauses and clauses into sentences. Modern logic attempts to combine composition and implication into a single deductive mechanism.

Broader

  • grammar

Related

  • implication
  • deduction

Notes

Example

A child who has never heard the sentence "The purple elephant did not sing, and the mouse did" understands it at once. The meaning is composed from the parts and the way they are put together.

History Note

  • George Boole reduces the laws of thought to an algebra of AND, OR, and NOT (The Laws of Thought, 1854). SEP: George Boole
  • Frege observes that a thought can be expressed by a sentence never heard before, because its parts correspond to the parts of the sentence ("Compound Thoughts", 1923); the principle of compositionality is named after him.
  • Wittgenstein sets out the truth-functional connectives in truth tables (Tractatus 4.31, 5.101).
  • Richard Montague treats English as a formal language whose meanings are composed by rule ("Universal Grammar", 1970).

Scope Note

Composition lets a finite vocabulary say an unbounded number of things. When logic folds composition and implication into one calculus, the truth table and the rule of inference become the same machine; that is the strength of formal logic and, under the Myth of the Proven, its temptation.
