Definition

Prejudice is the monster of the labyrinth of reasons that stops us at a bare assertion. A chain of reasons must begin somewhere, and where it begins is where the reasoner already stands: the unavoidable first-person perspective. We must always start in medias res.

It is the dogmatic break-off of the Münchhausen trilemma and the fourth mode of Agrippa, hypothesis.

Broader

  • labyrinth of reasons

Notes

Alternative Label

  • perspective

Example

"The prejudices of the individual, far more than his judgments, constitute the historical reality of his being."

Gadamer, Truth and Method, Part II (trans. Weinsheimer and Marshall)

History Note

  • Agrippa's fourth mode, hypothesis: the dogmatist, driven back without end, starts from something he does not establish but simply assumes (Sextos Empeirikos, PH I.168).
  • Francis Bacon's idols of the tribe, the cave, the marketplace, and the theater are the prejudices a new science must clear away (Novum Organum I.38–68).
  • The Enlightenment set reason against prejudice; Gadamer rehabilitates prejudice (Vorurteil, "pre-judgment") as the condition of all understanding (Truth and Method, 1960). SEP: Hans-Georg Gadamer
  • Hans Albert's Münchhausen trilemma (1968) makes the dogmatic break-off one of the three ends of every attempt at ultimate justification.

Scope Note

The Badinagean prejudice is closer to Gadamer's than to Bacon's. It names where reasoning starts, not a bias that better reasoning removes; subjectivity cannot reason from nowhere, and we can never escape our own biases and illusions, only examine portions not yet examined. Prejudice leads astray only when it forgets that it is a starting point and passes itself off as a proof.
