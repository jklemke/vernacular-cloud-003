Definition

Absolute Justice is the puzzle that reason meets when it asks, "Is there an ultimate tribunal?"

Thesis: there is an absolute justice, a final tribunal, whether of God or of reason, whose verdict settles what is right.

Antithesis: every tribunal is a human institution, and its legitimacy must itself be judged.

It is one of the three antinomies Badinage adds to Kant's four.

Notes

Example

"The strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must."

The Athenian envoys to the Melians (Thucydides V.89)

"The powerful attempt to impose virtue on the powerless, while the powerless attempt to impose justice on the powerful."

Victor Badinage

History Note

  • Plato's Socrates asks Euthyphro whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or is pious because the gods love it (Euthyph. 10a). Wikipedia: Euthyphro dilemma
  • In the Republic, Thrasymachus holds that justice is the advantage of the stronger, and Glaucon's ring of Gyges asks who would be just if he could not be seen (Rep. 338c, 359c–360d).
  • Kant postulates God and immortality so that virtue and happiness may at last coincide in the highest good (KpV 5:122–132).
  • William James: belief in God lets us take a "moral holiday" (Pragmatism, 1907, Lecture II).

Scope Note

Justice is the grammaticalization of social practice. The divine tribunal is the oubliation of justice on earth: justice delayed until an imaginary tribunal in the afterlife buys purpose in the here and now, and lets the powerful take a moral holiday. Absolute justice is closely tied to the idea of a just God and of absolute morality. The question it leaves is how we legitimize human jurisprudence.
