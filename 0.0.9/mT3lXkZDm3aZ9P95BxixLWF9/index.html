Definition

A magisterium is a contested space of questions that no single discipline can settle: one of the truly transcendent topics of philosophy. The nine Magisteria are IPSEITY, WORLD, SOCIETY, LANGUAGE, CONTINGENCY, ENTIRETY, INFINITY, MORALITY, and EPIPHANY. Each carries two conceptual devices.

The word is Stephen Jay Gould's, but the scope is broader. Gould gave science and religion each a magisterium of its own, and declared that they do not overlap. Badinage's magisteria are many, and they overlap: they share concepts across their devices, and the puzzles of the Antinomies are asked within them.

Example

Is there a self? Did the world begin? Where does counting end? By what rule should I act? No laboratory and no seminary settles any of these.

History Note

  • Latin magisterium, the office of a magister (teacher); in Catholic usage, the Church's teaching authority. Wikipedia: Magisterium
  • Christian Wolff divides metaphysics into general (ontology) and special: rational psychology, cosmology, and natural theology (Philosophia prima, sive Ontologia, 1730). Wikipedia: Christian Wolff
  • Kant turns Wolff's special metaphysics into the three ideas of pure reason, the soul, the world, and God, and shows where reason overreaches in each (KrV A334–338/B391–396).
  • Stephen Jay Gould, "Nonoverlapping Magisteria" (Natural History 106, March 1997), then Rocks of Ages (1999). Wikipedia: Non-overlapping magisteria

Scope Note

The Magisteria are not disciplines, and they are not institutions: schools, courts, and markets belong to the Jurisdictions. Seven of them are home to an antinomy: Absolute Perspective (IPSEITY), Absolute Beginning (WORLD), Absolute Grammar (LANGUAGE), Absolute Necessity (CONTINGENCY), Absolute Description (ENTIRETY), Absolute Granularity (INFINITY), and Absolute Justice (MORALITY). The names are written in capitals to mark them as philosophy's transcendent questions. The list is a snapshot for our day, not a deduction.
