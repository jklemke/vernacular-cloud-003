Definition

Sociality is a practice in service of norms held in common, first of all survival, flourishing, and reality: what an enactive agent does among other agents like itself. Its working assumption is the mind-dependent notion of mind-independent norms (MDNMIN).

Loyalty, empathy, and fairness appear before there is any word for them. A social creature takes the norms it shares to bind others as well as itself, and in sociality legitimacy first has something to work on.

Example

Vampire bats share blood with roost-mates who failed to feed, and they remember who shared with them.

History Note

  • Aristotle: the human being is by nature a political animal (Politics I.2, 1253a2–3). Wikipedia: Politics (Aristotle)
  • Gerald Wilkinson documents reciprocal blood sharing among vampire bats (Nature, 1984). Wikipedia: Reciprocal altruism
  • Richard Rorty proposes solidarity in place of objectivity ("Solidarity or Objectivity?", 1985). Wikipedia: Richard Rorty
  • Michael Tomasello: shared intentionality is what sets human cooperation apart (A Natural History of Human Thinking, 2014). Wikipedia: Shared intentionality

Scope Note

Social practice is not discredited by calling it the Myth of the Coven. Shared norms do not replace a shared world: sociality presupposes reality; it does not manufacture it. Sociality can be carried out without language, though language transforms it.
