Definition

Predication is the grammatical operation of saying something about what has been indicated. Indication is what we are talking about; predication is what we are saying about it. Analytic philosophy has concentrated its attention on predication; Badinage pairs it with indication.

Notes

Example

The Eleatic Stranger offers Theaetetus two sentences: "Theaetetus sits" and "Theaetetus, with whom I am now talking, flies." Both are about the same thing; the first says what is, the second what is not (Plato, Sophist 263a–b).

History Note

  • Plato's Sophist finds the simplest sentence in the weaving of a name with a verb (Soph. 261d–263d).
  • Aristotle distinguishes what is said of a subject from what is in a subject, and sorts predicates into ten categories (Cat. 2–4).
  • Frege analyzes predicates as unsaturated concepts, functions from objects to truth values ("Function and Concept", 1891; "On Concept and Object", 1892). SEP: Frege
  • P. F. Strawson asks why subject and predicate are asymmetrical (Individuals, 1959).
  • Donald Davidson calls the unity of the proposition "the problem of predication" (Truth and Predication, 2005).

Scope Note

Predication carries the problem of universals: what is said of many things at once. In the Editorial Note's terms, indication picks out the this (haecceity) and predication the what (quiddity).
