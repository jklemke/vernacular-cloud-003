Definition

Suspension, ostension, and transcendence are a conceptual device of EPIPHANY: three registers of language, each with its own sense of existence, truth, and knowledge. Ostension points to what is there; suspension holds a belief in a structured field of foundational, correspondent, and coherent forces, without grounding it; transcendence rises above, to the general and the metaphorical.

We don't want to use the same notion of existence, truth, and knowledge across these three registers. To do so is the Socratic Miscarriage.

History Note

  • Hegel's Aufhebung, to cancel, preserve, and lift up at once (Science of Logic, 1812); Badinage renders it "suspension".
  • Wittgenstein on the limits of ostensive definition (Philosophical Investigations §§28–30, 1953).
  • Otto Neurath: we are like sailors who must rebuild their ship on the open sea ("Protokollsätze", 1932). Wikipedia: Neurath's boat
  • Quine's web of belief ("Two Dogmas of Empiricism", 1951).

Scope Note

Suspension here is the epistemic sense of suspension: belief neither grounded nor groundless, but held. The Example shows all three registers in one stone: the stone pointed to, the stone believed to have heard, the stone that stands for a covenant.
