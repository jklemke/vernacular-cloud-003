Definition

We enter the labyrinth whenever we try to define the Unsayables or to justify everything in words. The labyrinth is home not to a mere Minotaur but to seven monsters: circularity, proliferation, prejudice, disputation, subterfuge, ambiguity, and oubliation.

The labyrinth of reasons is just one of the many ways philosophy can lead us astray. Some philosophers love to explore the labyrinth, some refuse to enter it, and the therapeutic hermeneuticist enters it looking for the clew, Ariadne's thread, that leads back out.

Wilfrid Sellars called it the space of reasons; Robert Brandom made it a space of norms; Badinage calls it a labyrinth, because it has more entrances than exits. Wittgenstein was optimistic when he compared it to a fly bottle.

History Note

  • Daedalus builds the labyrinth on Crete for King Minos to hide the Minotaur; Ariadne gives Theseus a clew of thread to find his way back out (Plutarch, Thes. 15–19; Apollodoros, Bibl. III.15.8 and Epit. 1.7–9). Wikipedia: Labyrinth
  • The five modes of Agrippa (dissent, regress, relativity, hypothesis, circularity) survive in Sextos Empeirikos (PH I.164–169) and Diogenes Laertios (Lives IX.88–89). Wikipedia: Agrippa the Skeptic
  • Hans Albert names the Münchhausen trilemma (Traktat über kritische Vernunft, 1968): every attempt at ultimate justification ends in an infinite regress, a logical circle, or a dogmatic break-off. Wikipedia: Münchhausen trilemma
  • Wilfrid Sellars places knowing "in the logical space of reasons, of justifying and being able to justify what one says" (EPM §36); Robert Brandom recasts that space as a social practice governed by norms, "the game of giving and asking for reasons" (Making It Explicit, 1994). SEP: Wilfrid Sellars
  • Wittgenstein calls language a labyrinth of paths (PI §203) and philosophy a battle against the bewitchment of our intelligence by means of language (PI §109).

Scope Note

The labyrinth is not simply a catalogue of fallacies. Some of its paths are errors that a better argument avoids; others are where even the best argument ends when it is asked to justify everything. Antiquity counted five modes of Agrippa and modernity a trilemma; Badinage, for numerological profundity, counts seven monsters: three from the Münchhausen trilemma (circularity, proliferation, prejudice), two more from the modes of Agrippa (disputation, ambiguity), and two of his own (subterfuge, oubliation).
