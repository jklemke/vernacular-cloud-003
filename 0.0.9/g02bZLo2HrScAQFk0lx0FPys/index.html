Definition

Indication is the grammatical operation of pointing out what we are talking about: an object, an event, a situation, an isolate, an aggregate, a parameter, a conjecture. Indication is what we are talking about; predication is what we are saying about it.

Badinage says "indication" where grammarians say noun phrase, nominative case, or the subject of a sentence, as a deliberate parallel to the analytic philosopher's focus on predication.

Notes

Example

The table is brown. The brown table is wooden. That is a brown table. That brown object is a table.

"The dog" is a definite indication; "a dog" is an indefinite indication.

History Note

  • Bertrand Russell analyzes "denoting phrases" such as "the present King of France" as disguised descriptions ("On Denoting", 1905). SEP: Descriptions
  • P. F. Strawson answers that referring is something speakers do with expressions, not something expressions do by themselves ("On Referring", 1950).
  • Quine separates reference from denotation and distinguishes referential from non-referential occurrences of terms ("Reference and Modality", 1953).
  • Wilfrid Sellars distinguishes picturing, a natural-order relation between linguistic objects and the world, from signifying (Science and Metaphysics, 1968, ch. V).
  • Natural Semantic Metalanguage counts THIS, SOMEONE, and SOMETHING~THING among its semantic primes.

Scope Note

A situation is an ontological object, a noun is a linguistic object, but indication is a grammatical operation. Its forms include ostensive, descriptive, and parameterized indication, and it carries the distinction between de re and de dicto. In the indicative mood the whole sentence points.
