Definition

Sapience is a practice in service of the norms of truth and meaning: roughly, the use of ostensive language, a fully grammatical language that refers to actual and possible situations in a shared environment. It is the first of the Practical Realms that depends on language.

With sapience, what a creature does includes what it says: asserting, asking, warning, promising. Its working assumption is the mind-dependent notion of mind-independent concepts (MDNMIC).

History Note

  • Protagoras: man is the measure of all things (reported in Plato, Theaetetus 152a). Wikipedia: Homo mensura
  • Peirce makes truth the opinion that inquiry is fated to reach ("How to Make Our Ideas Clear", 1878); William James answers that truth happens to an idea (Pragmatism, 1907, Lecture VI). Wikipedia: Pragmatic theory of truth
  • J. L. Austin: to say something is often to do something (How to Do Things with Words, 1962). Wikipedia: Speech act
  • Paul Grice separates what is said from what is implicated ("Logic and Conversation", 1975). Wikipedia: Cooperative principle

Scope Note

Sapient praxis is not discredited by calling it the Myth of the Proven. Sapience is not intelligence in general, nor reason set over against the senses: it grows out of sentience, enaction, and sociality and answers to them. Pragmatism, rightly understood, is not a theory of truth; it is a theory about the legitimacy of corroboration.
