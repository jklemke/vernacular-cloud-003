Definition

Absolute Granularity is the puzzle that reason meets when it asks, "Where are the boundaries?"

Thesis: the world is made of simple parts, with sharp boundaries between one thing and the next.

Antithesis: every part can be divided again, and every boundary blurs when we look closely.

It is Kant's second antinomy, and it is at once an epistemological, a physical, and a metaphysical boundary.

Notes

Example

One grain of sand is not a heap. Adding one grain to something that is not a heap never makes it a heap. So there are no heaps.

The Athenians kept the ship of Theseus for centuries, replacing each rotten plank with a new one, until philosophers argued whether it was still the same ship (Plutarch, Thes. 23).

History Note

  • Eubulides of Miletus is credited with the sorites, the paradox of the heap (Diogenes Laertios, Lives II.108). SEP: Sorites Paradox
  • Plutarch records the Ship of Theseus as a stock puzzle of the philosophers (Thes. 23).
  • Kant's second antinomy: every composite substance consists of simple parts; no composite thing consists of simple parts (KrV A434/B462).
  • Derek Parfit argues that personal identity is not what matters in survival (Reasons and Persons, 1984).
  • Timothy Williamson defends epistemicism: vague predicates have sharp boundaries that we cannot know (Vagueness, 1994).

Scope Note

Vagueness, the sorites, the Ship of Theseus, personal identity, and species boundaries are one antinomy seen at different scales. What we have is a taxonomy of first-person instruments, the interaction of many localized structural-functional organizations: the bee and the flower, the seed and the squirrel, the fruit and the parrot, each sorting the other, according to its norms, into categories of compatibility and incompatibility. Granularity is combinatorially explosive, so we must oubliate whatever is not being actively discerned.
