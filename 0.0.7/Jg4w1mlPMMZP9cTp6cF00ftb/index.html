Definition

MORALITY is the contested space of questions like "By what rule?" Morality is a transcendent, a priori, presuppositional norm of sentient, social, and sapient practice: the general perspective, but not the Absolute Perspective. Its conceptual devices are loyalty-empathy-fairness and liberty-governance-revolt.

We need no moral facts and no objective morality. Norms are not facts, and morality is neither merely expressive nor merely sentient. Moral discourse lies in the conceptualization, the articulation, and the case-by-case application of the norm: what rule am I claiming to act by, and do my actions keep to it? Subterfuge, sanctimony, and hypocrisy are its failures.

History Note

  • Hume: no ought follows from an is (Treatise of Human Nature 3.1.1, 1739).
  • Kant: act only on a maxim you can at the same time will as a universal law (Groundwork 4:421, 1785).
  • Hegel's lord and bondsman (Phenomenology of Spirit §§178–196, 1807).
  • William James's "moral holiday" (Pragmatism, Lecture II, 1907; The Meaning of Truth, 1909).
  • J. L. Mackie: there are no objective values (Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong, 1977). SEP: Moral Anti-Realism

Scope Note

MORALITY is not moral realism, and it is not expressivism. Nor is it Kant's universal law: the question is not whether a rule could be universal, but whether I keep the rule I claim. The institutions of moral judgement belong to jurisprudence among the Jurisdictions. The antinomy at home in MORALITY is Absolute Justice.
