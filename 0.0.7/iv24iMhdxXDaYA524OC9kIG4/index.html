Definition

CONTINGENCY is the contested space of questions like "What could have been otherwise?" It is the arrow of time: the actualization of one outcome from countless preconditions within constrained possibilities. Its conceptual devices are compatibility-incompatibility-consequence and possible-impossible-necessary.

Badinage deprecates the word "cause". A cause is an abstract, type-level pattern; the contingent is a concrete, token-level actualization. The puzzle of determinism and free will is dissolved rather than decided: there is room for spontaneous, indexical, localized actualization both in what happens and in what is done.

Example

We should strive to keep the elusive word "causality" out of our fundamental discourse. But if we substitute "contingent" are we not just inserting another elusive word? Maybe so. But at least this word proclaims its shiftiness boldy on its surface, rather than hiding it behind a facade of precision.

Victor Badinage

History Note

  • Aristotle's sea battle: a statement about tomorrow's sea battle is not yet true or false (De Interpretatione 9, 18a28–19b4).
  • Hume finds constant conjunction but no necessary connection between events (Enquiry concerning Human Understanding VII, 1748).
  • Peirce's tychism: absolute chance is real in nature ("The Doctrine of Necessity Examined", 1892). SEP: Charles Sanders Peirce
  • Russell argues that the word "cause" should be extruded from the philosophical vocabulary ("On the Notion of Cause", 1912–13). Wikipedia: Causality

Scope Note

CONTINGENCY is neither determinism nor indeterminism. What happens and what is done are distinguished as in the Practical Realms (HAPPEN and DO). Hume found constant conjunction; Badinage finds only occasional conjunction. The antinomy at home in CONTINGENCY is Absolute Necessity.
