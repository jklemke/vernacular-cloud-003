Definition

A category is a conceptual device: a triad of interdependent concepts, each best explained in relation to the other two. Reality is plural, and a triad is the minimal model of a plurality. The eighteen Categories are carried two by two by the nine Magisteria.

To build such devices is conceptual engineering: not discovering the one true table of categories, but designing a vocabulary fit for its work. The list is not deduced, as Kant tried to deduce his; it is a snapshot for our day, offered with what Badinage hopes is a better ear for coinage than Aristotle, Kant, or Peirce had.

Example

Possible, impossible, necessary: none of the three can be explained without the other two.

History Note

  • Aristotle lists ten categories: substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, state, action, and affection (Categories 4, 1b25–2a4). SEP: Aristotle's Categories
  • Kant derives twelve categories from the table of judgments, in four groups of three; the third in each group arises from combining the first two (KrV A80/B106, B110). Wikipedia: Category (Kant)
  • Peirce reduces the categories to three, later called Firstness, Secondness, and Thirdness, and holds that every relation reduces to triads while no triad reduces to pairs ("On a New List of Categories", 1867). SEP: Charles Sanders Peirce
  • Carnap's explication replaces a vague concept with an exact one fit for a purpose (Logical Foundations of Probability, 1950, §2); Herman Cappelen calls the program conceptual engineering (Fixing Language, 2018). Wikipedia: Conceptual engineering

Scope Note

The Categories are not Aristotle's kinds of predicate, nor Kant's pure concepts of the understanding, though some echo them: possible-impossible-necessary is nearly Kant's Modality. Kant's deduction of his categories is widely judged not to succeed, and Badinage does not attempt one. Conceptual devices not yet placed under a magisterium are kept under device.
