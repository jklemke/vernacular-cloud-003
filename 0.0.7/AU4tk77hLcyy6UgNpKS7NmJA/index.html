Definition

A jurisdiction is an institutionalized social arena where judgments are rendered, participation is offered, legitimacy is claimed, and tradition is valued. Its working notions are the tribunal, the office, the court of public opinion, governance, regulation, and oversight.

Where the Physical Realms interpret a mind-independent world, roughly the Naturwissenschaften, the Jurisdictions are the Geisteswissenschaften: technology, art, mathematics, commerce, jurisprudence, ideology, and education. The Magisteria pose the questions; the Jurisdictions claim the authority to answer them.

Example

The word "true" is not redundant. To say that "Snow is white" is true, is to imply that it has been declared true in some legitimated tribunal. This is the grammatical function of stipulation.

Victor Badinage

History Note

  • Wilhelm Dilthey separates the human sciences from the natural sciences (Einleitung in die Geisteswissenschaften, 1883). SEP: Wilhelm Dilthey
  • Wilhelm von Humboldt's University of Berlin unites research and teaching (1810). Wikipedia: Humboldtian model of higher education
  • Max Weber: the state claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force ("Politics as a Vocation", 1919).
  • C. P. Snow's two cultures, the literary and the scientific (Rede Lecture, 1959). Wikipedia: The Two Cultures
  • Jürgen Habermas on the public sphere (The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere, 1962); Karl Popper's World 3 of objective knowledge (Objective Knowledge, 1972).

Scope Note

The Jurisdictions are not academic disciplines, though the disciplines defined in the nineteenth century are one institutional form of them. Nor are they the Practical Realms: a practice becomes a jurisdiction when it is institutionalized under an authority that claims legitimacy. At this level of abstraction science, religion, philosophy, and ritual all sit under ideology.
