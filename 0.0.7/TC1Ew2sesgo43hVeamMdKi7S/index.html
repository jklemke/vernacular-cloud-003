Definition

Telos (τέλος) is the Greek word for end: the finish of something, its fulfilment, and its purpose. It could also mean a rite, an office, a death, and a tax.

For Aristotle the telos is "that for the sake of which" a thing is or is done, one of the four ways of explaining it: the acorn is for the oak, the knife for cutting, the heart for pumping blood. Christianity made it divine purpose; modern science tried to do without it.

Example

Πᾶσα τέχνη καὶ πᾶσα μέθοδος, ὁμοίως δὲ πρᾶξίς τε καὶ προαίρεσις, ἀγαθοῦ τινὸς ἐφίεσθαι δοκεῖ.

"Every craft and every inquiry, and likewise every action and choice, seems to aim at some good."

Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics I.1, 1094a1–2

History Note

  • Aristotle's fourth kind of explanation (aitia, traditionally "cause" in English) is "that for the sake of which" (τὸ οὗ ἕνεκα) (Physics II.3, 194b32–35); nature acts for an end (Physics II.8).
  • The everyday senses stay alive beside the philosophical one: in the Gospels telos also means a tax or toll (Matthew 17:25), and Paul's "Then comes the end" (εἶτα τὸ τέλος, 1 Corinthians 15:24, NRSVue) makes it the end of history.
  • Latin renders it finis and causa finalis; Arabic ghāya. Christian Wolff coins teleologia (1728). Wikipedia: Teleology
  • Early modern mechanism expels final explanations ("final causes") from physics; biology readmits them as function, and Colin Pittendrigh proposes "teleonomy" (1958) for purpose without design.
  • Not to be confused with the tele- of telescope and telephone, from τῆλε, "far".

Scope Note

Telos is not design, and in this nomenclature nothing is for anything until there is metabolism: survival is the first norm, the first end that anything has. The Norms are what remains of telos once it has been taken back from the heavens and returned to living things.
