Definition

Gramma (γράμμα) is the Greek word for a written mark: a letter of the alphabet, a line in a drawing, and in the plural (grammata) writing, letters, learning. It comes from graphein, to scratch, to draw, to write.

The art of letters was grammatikē, and grammar is its descendant. Plato's Socrates feared that letters would breed forgetfulness, and Paul set the letter against the spirit: "the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life".

History Note

  • In the Phaedrus, the god Theuth offers letters (grammata) as a drug for memory; King Thamus answers that they will bring forgetfulness (lēthē) into souls, and only the appearance of wisdom (274c–275b).
  • In the Cratylus, Socrates plays with the idea that letters and sounds imitate what they name, and that names are built from them (422e–427c).
  • Paul sets gramma against pneuma (2 Corinthians 3:6); Origen builds on the contrast a reading of Scripture by its literal and its spiritual senses (On First Principles IV).
  • Latin renders it littera (litterae, letters, literature) and the art grammatica; Arabic ḥarf (letter) and naḥw (grammar). Wikipedia: Grammar
  • Modern descendants: grammar, diagram, program, telegram, the gram (a small weight, via French gramme, 1795), and, through Scots, glamour, once a word for occult learning. Derrida's grammatology sets writing against the priority of speech (De la grammatologie, 1967).

Scope Note

Gramma is not the grammar of this nomenclature, which names the boundary conditions of a fully grammatical language rather than its letters. The quarrel of letter and spirit is older than the nomenclature and runs through it: the Myth of the Scriven is the myth that the written word carries its own authority. Grammomorphic is a provisional coinage of Badinage's for what takes the shape of grammar.
