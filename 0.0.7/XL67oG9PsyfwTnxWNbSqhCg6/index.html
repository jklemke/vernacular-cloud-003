Definition

First person, second person, and third person are a conceptual device of SOCIETY: the first person is the referent of "I", the second person the referent of "you", and the third person the referent of "he", "she", and "they". A society is held together by a grammar with words for all three.

To speak in the first person is to avow; in the second, to address; in the third, to describe. Greek has them as egō, sy, and autoi.

History Note

  • Émile Benveniste: "I" and "you" are persons, while the third person is a "non-person" ("Relationships of Person in the Verb", 1946; Problems in General Linguistics, 1966). Wikipedia: Émile Benveniste
  • Martin Buber's I–Thou and I–It (Ich und Du, 1923).
  • Daniel Dennett's heterophenomenology, a third-person method for first-person reports (Consciousness Explained, 1991). Wikipedia: Heterophenomenology
  • Stephen Darwall grounds moral obligation in the second-person standpoint (The Second-Person Standpoint, 2006).

Scope Note

The device is the grammar of person, not the persons. It echoes, lightly, Peirce's Firstness, Secondness, and Thirdness. The hard problem of the first person belongs to the ego trope.
