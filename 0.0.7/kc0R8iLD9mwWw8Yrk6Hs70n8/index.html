Definition

Pattern is the ineffable notion of regularity: the same again, form that recurs across difference. We find patterns, make them, and name them, yet every attempt to say what a pattern is reaches for form, information, structure, or law, and each of these is already a pattern.

Like each of the Unsayables, pattern cannot be defined; we must explicate it by use, in the prototype sentence: Subjectivity detects patterns of isolates, aggregates, and voids in time and space.

Example

A melody stays the same melody in another key, on another instrument, or hummed: every note changes, and the pattern does not.

History Note

  • Plato's forms (eidos) are the patterns in which particular things participate. Wikipedia: Theory of forms
  • Aristotle's hylomorphism joins form (morphē) to matter (hylē). Wikipedia: Hylomorphism
  • Hume finds only constant conjunction, never necessary connection; induction rests on custom (Enquiry concerning Human Understanding §§4–7).
  • Russell restates the problem of induction: the future need not resemble the past (Problems of Philosophy, ch. 6).
  • Bateson defines information as "a difference which makes a difference" (Steps to an Ecology of Mind, 1972).

Scope Note

A rule or law of nature is a pattern that has been conceptualized, articulated, and grammaticalized. Higher-level patterns obey lower-level laws, but are not derivable from them in any tractable or explanatory way. The future is like the past in some ways and unlike it in others; we sort situations by how repetitive they are.
