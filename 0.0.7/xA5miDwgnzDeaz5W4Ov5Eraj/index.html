Definition

Hylē (ὕλη) is the Greek word for wood: forest, timber, firewood. Aristotle made it the word for matter, what a thing is made from, as a table is made from timber. Latin did the same with materia, which also first meant timber.

Every natural thing, for Aristotle, is matter shaped by form (morphē); this is hylomorphism. The Gnostics later ranked people as material (hylikoi), ensouled (psychikoi), and spiritual (pneumatikoi).

Example

οἷα τέκτοσιν ἡμῖν ὕλη παράκειται.

"Like carpenters, we have our materials (hylē, timber) lying ready."

Plato, Timaeus 69a

History Note

  • In Homer, hylē is woodland and forest (Iliad 11.155).
  • Plato's Timaeus still uses it as a carpenter's metaphor (69a). Aristotle makes it a technical term: that out of which a thing comes to be and which remains in it, such as the bronze of a statue (Physics II.3, 194b23–24).
  • The Valentinian Gnostics divide humankind into hylikoi, psychikoi, and pneumatikoi (Irenaeus, Against Heresies I.7.5).
  • Latin renders it materia (timber, then matter); Arabic borrows it as hayūlā, beside the native mādda. Wikipedia: Hylomorphism
  • Modern descendants are scholarly: hylomorphism; hylozoism, coined by Ralph Cudworth (1678) for the doctrine that matter is alive.

Scope Note

Hylē is not the matter of modern physics, a quantity of mass-energy; it is always the matter of something, ready to be shaped. In this nomenclature matter is the framework of molecularity, and what a thing is made of depends on how it is taken: as an isolate or an aggregate.
