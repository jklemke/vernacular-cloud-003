Definition

Mythos (μῦθος) is the Greek word for word, speech, and story. In Homer it is simply what someone says, often a speech of authority.

Only later is mythos set against logos, the tale against the reasoned account, and only in modern languages does "myth" come to mean a falsehood. Plato banished the poets' myths from his city and then told myths of his own.

Example

"A poet, if he is to be a poet, must make myths, not arguments."

Socrates in prison, Plato, Phaedo 61b

History Note

  • In Homer, mythos is speech, often command: Agamemnon dismisses the priest Chryses with "a harsh word" (κρατερὸν ... μῦθον, Iliad 1.25).
  • Plato censors the poets' myths (Republic II–III, 377a ff.) and ends the Republic with a myth of his own, the myth of Er (X, 614b ff.).
  • Aristotle makes mythos the plot of a tragedy, "its first principle and, as it were, its soul" (Poetics 6, 1450a38).
  • Latin renders it fabula. The Quran reports unbelievers dismissing revelation as "tales of the ancients" (asāṭīr al-awwalīn, e.g. 6:25).
  • Modern descendants: myth, mythology. Nietzsche, Freud, and structural anthropology rehabilitate myth; Rudolf Bultmann proposes to "demythologize" the New Testament (1941). Wikipedia: Myth

Scope Note

Mythos is not falsehood. In this nomenclature a myth is a narrative, transcendent explanation that carries an edifying message whether or not it is true, and the seven Myths are the varieties of metaphysics. The opposition of mythos and logos is itself one of them: the Myth of the Proven is the myth that certainty can replace the need for myth.
