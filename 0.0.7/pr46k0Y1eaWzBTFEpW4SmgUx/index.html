Definition

Eidos (εἶδος) is the Greek word for what is seen: the look or shape of a thing, and so its kind. It shares a root with idea (ἰδέα) and with Latin videre, to see.

Plato made the eidē the Forms, eternal and apart from things; Aristotle put form back into things, as what makes this bronze a statue and this flesh a horse. The two never agreed, and the word kept both meanings, and a third besides: through Porphyry, eidos became the logician's species.

Example

εἶδος γάρ πού τι ἓν ἕκαστον εἰώθαμεν τίθεσθαι περὶ ἕκαστα τὰ πολλά, οἷς ταὐτὸν ὄνομα ἐπιφέρομεν.

"We are accustomed to positing a single form for each set of many things to which we apply the same name."

Plato, Republic X, 596a

History Note

  • In Homer and in everyday Greek, eidos is a person's looks, appearance, build.
  • Plato's Forms: beauty itself, by which beautiful things are beautiful (Phaedo 100b–e); one form for each name (Republic X, 596a). Plato raises the strongest objections himself, among them the regress later called the third man (Parmenides 130b–135c).
  • Aristotle rejects separate Forms (Metaphysics A.9) and makes form what is realized in matter (Metaphysics Z.8; Physics II.1). Wikipedia: Theory of forms
  • Porphyry's Isagoge makes eidos the species under a genus, and through Boethius's Latin (species) sets the medieval problem of universals. Latin also renders it forma; Arabic ṣūra (form) and nawʿ (species).
  • Modern descendants: idea, ideal, idol (from eidōlon, image), kaleidoscope (David Brewster, 1817), the suffix -oid. Husserl's eidetic reduction seeks the essence of what appears (Ideas I, 1913).

Scope Note

Eidos is not one doctrine but at least three: Plato's separate Form, Aristotle's form in matter, and the logician's species. Plato's own answer to the Cratylus's question is here: a name names a Form. In this nomenclature a concept emerges in use and is held in place by corroboration; what Plato called a Form is closer to a pattern that sentience has learned to select.
