Definition

Pneuma (πνεῦμα) is the Greek word for wind and breath, and then for spirit. In Greek medicine it is the vital air carried in the body; for the Stoics, a fiery breath that holds the cosmos together; in the Septuagint and the New Testament, the spirit of God, and then the Holy Spirit.

Pneuma and psychē both begin as breath, and the line between them has never been drawn the same way twice. In the Gospel of John a single sentence uses pneuma for the wind and for the Spirit.

History Note

  • Anaximenes, as reported: "as our soul, being air, holds us together, so breath (pneuma) and air surround the whole world" (fr. B2): psychē and pneuma in one sentence at the start.
  • Greek medicine carries pneuma in the arteries; Galen distinguishes a vital from a psychic pneuma, the "spirits" that survive into Descartes' animal spirits. The Stoics make pneuma a tensed, fiery breath that pervades and holds the cosmos together. Wikipedia: Pneuma
  • The Septuagint renders Hebrew ruach, wind, breath, spirit, as pneuma: πνεῦμα θεοῦ ἐπεφέρετο ἐπάνω τοῦ ὕδατος (Genesis 1:2). In the New Testament it becomes the Holy Spirit, the Paraclete (John 14:26), and Paul's pneumatikos, the spiritual person (1 Corinthians 2:15).
  • Latin renders it spiritus (also breath); Arabic rūḥ. Modern descendants: pneumatic, pneumonia, pneumatology; and, through Latin, spirit, spiritual, inspire, expire.

Scope Note

Pneuma is not a ghost in the machine. In this nomenclature breath belongs to metabolism and the life of the body, the "spirit" of an encounter to sentience and transcendence, and the Spirit as Paraclete to the Names of God, as the comforter. That one Greek word could cover all of these is the point of this note.
