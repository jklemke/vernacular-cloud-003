Definition

Stipulation is the grammatical operation of making a statement about a statement: Alice believes that x; Boris asserts that x; Carlos thinks that x; Doris knows that x. It is metalinguistic, a pointer to a supra-suspensional linguistic realm. Truth is a stipulative grammatical construct.

Example

"This sentence is unprovable."

The Gödel sentence, in Hofstadter's plain-English form

"Stipulation is a metalinguistic feature of grammar."

Victor Badinage

History Note

  • Frege: in reported speech and belief contexts, words refer not to their usual referents but to their senses ("On Sense and Reference", 1892).
  • Russell names the "propositional attitudes", believing, desiring, and the like ("The Philosophy of Logical Atomism", 1918; An Inquiry into Meaning and Truth, 1940). SEP: Propositional Attitude Reports
  • Tarski separates the object language from the metalanguage in which its truth is defined (1933).
  • Gödel constructs a sentence of arithmetic that says, in effect, that it is not provable (1931).
  • Douglas Hofstadter makes such self-reference the "strange loop" at the heart of the self (Gödel, Escher, Bach, 1979).

Scope Note

The word "true" is not redundant: to say that "Snow is white" is true is to say that it has been declared true in some legitimated tribunal. This is the grammatical function of stipulation. We still need to investigate the strange loop of talking about talking, and the discovery of the logical necessity of metalinguistic stipulation.
