Definition

The Unsayables are seven terms that every philosophical system presupposes and that none can define: time, space, pattern, isolate, aggregate, void, and subjectivity. They are the unexplained explainers of the Badinagean nomenclature.

Since they cannot be defined, we must explicate them by use, in a prototype sentence that uses all seven and defines none:

Subjectivity detects patterns of isolates, aggregates, and voids in time and space.

Any attempt to define these terms will lead us into the labyrinth of reasons.

Example

"What then is time? If no one asks me, I know; if I wish to explain it to one who asks, I know not."

Augustine, Conf. XI.14.17

"Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent."

Wittgenstein, TLP 7 (trans. Ogden)

The Unsayables survive every skeptical scenario: Plato's cave, the brain in a vat, The Matrix, The Truman Show. Each changes the subject's situated, indexical perspective; none changes time, space, or subjectivity.

Scope Note

The Unsayables are not offered as perennial, necessary, or absolute. They are a working set of primitives: we see what follows if we take them as undefined, and we find that a smaller set leads into quandaries. They form an interdependent closure set; each becomes meaningful only through the others. Attempts to articulate them tend to slide into objectification, occultation, and oubliation.

History Note

  • Plato's Seventh Letter says the highest matters cannot be put into words as other studies can (Ep. VII 341c–d). Wikipedia: Seventh Letter
  • The apophatic tradition, from Proklos and Pseudo-Dionysius through Christian, Jewish, and Islamic mysticism, speaks of the first principle only by negation. Wikipedia: Apophatic theology, SEP: Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite
  • Augustine knows what time is until someone asks him (Conf. XI.14.17; see Example).
  • Kant makes space and time the a priori forms of intuition, prior to all experience. The Unsayables extend his two forms to seven. SEP: Kant's Views on Space and Time
  • Wittgenstein ends the Tractatus with a call for silence (TLP 7; see Example).
  • Epistemology and metaphysics meet the same problem as foundations, grounding, and the regress of reasons: a chain of justification goes on forever, returns upon itself, or stops. labyrinth of reasons, Wikipedia: Agrippa the Skeptic, the five modes, SEP: Metaphysical Grounding
