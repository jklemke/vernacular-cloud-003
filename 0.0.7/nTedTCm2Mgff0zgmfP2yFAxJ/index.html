Definition

Praxis (πρᾶξις) is the Greek word for action, doing, a transaction, and, in the plural, one's affairs. Aristotle set it apart from poiēsis, making, and theōria, contemplation: in making, the end lies in the thing made; in praxis, acting well is itself the end.

The book of Acts is the Praxeis of the apostles. Marx made praxis the activity that changes the world rather than interpreting it.

Example

τῆς μὲν γὰρ ποιήσεως ἕτερον τὸ τέλος, τῆς δὲ πράξεως οὐκ ἂν εἴη· ἔστι γὰρ αὐτὴ ἡ εὐπραξία τέλος.

"For making has an end other than itself, but action does not: acting well is itself the end."

Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI.5, 1140b6–7

History Note

  • Aristotle divides knowledge into theoretical, practical, and productive (Metaphysics E.1, 1025b18–26), and sets praxis and its virtue, phronēsis, against making and its virtue, technē (Nicomachean Ethics VI.4–5).
  • The New Testament book of Acts is titled Praxeis Apostolōn, the doings of the apostles.
  • Latin renders it actio; Arabic ʿamal, as in the pairing of knowledge and action (ʿilm wa-ʿamal). From the related pragma (deed, thing) come pragmatic and pragmatism.
  • Marx: "The philosophers have only interpreted the world, in various ways; the point is to change it" (Theses on Feuerbach XI, 1845); Gramsci calls Marxism "the philosophy of praxis". Wikipedia: Praxis (process)
  • Modern descendants: practice, practical, praxis.

Scope Note

Praxis is not the "practice" of this nomenclature. The Practical Realms take their name from it, but a practice there is anything done in service of a norm, from nutrition to transcendence, and includes what Aristotle would have called making (technē). Aristotle's line between doing and making is one the nomenclature does not draw.
