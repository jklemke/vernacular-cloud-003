Definition

Archē (ἀρχή) is the Greek word for beginning, and also for first principle and for rule. One word holds when something starts, what it starts from, and who is in charge of it.

The first Greek philosophers asked what the archē of all things was: water, the boundless, air. The Septuagint and the Gospel of John both open with the word, and through Proklos and Pseudo-Dionysios it became the hierarchy of heaven, church, and state.

Example

Genesis 1:1 (NRSVue, from the Hebrew)

The Septuagint's Greek reads, literally: "In the beginning God made the sky and the earth."

History Note

  • Simplikios, reporting Theophrastos, says Anaximander was the first to call the underlying stuff of things the archē, and named it the boundless (apeiron) (In Physica 24.13–16). Wikipedia: Arche
  • Aristotle surveys his predecessors' first principles (Metaphysics A.3, 983b6 ff.) and lists the senses of archē, starting point, origin, ruler, premise, among others (Metaphysics Δ.1, 1012b34–1013a23).
  • Proklos derives all things in ordered series from one first principle, the One (Elements of Theology). Pseudo-Dionysios coins hierarchia (ἱεραρχία, "sacred rule") for the ranked orders of angels and clergy (Celestial Hierarchy; Ecclesiastical Hierarchy, c. 500). Wikipedia: Hierarchy
  • Latin renders archē as principium and initium; Arabic as mabdaʾ (plural mabādiʾ). Modern descendants: archaic, archetype, archive (from ἀρχεῖον, the magistrates' office), monarchy, anarchy, archbishop, hierarchy.

Scope Note

Archē is not one concept but three, and the slide between them does philosophical work: the first moment becomes the first principle, and the first principle becomes the rightful ruler. The search for a single archē meets Absolute Beginning, an antinomy; the nomenclature leaves beginnings among the Unsayables and names rule as legitimacy.
