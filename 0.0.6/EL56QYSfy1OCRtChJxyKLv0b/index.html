Definition

Dynamis (δύναμις) is the Greek word for power, capacity, and potentiality: what a thing can do or can become. Mathematicians used it for a square and its root; the Gospels for a miracle, a mighty work.

Aristotle paired it with energeia, actuality, to explain change without Parmenides' paradox: what comes to be was not nothing before, it was there potentially. Plotinos called the One the power of all things.

History Note

  • In the Theaetetus, Theodoros and the young Theaetetus call the irrational square roots dynameis, "powers" (147d–148b).
  • Aristotle's Metaphysics Θ is a book on dynamis and energeia; change is the actuality of what is potential, as such (Physics III.1, 201a10–11). Wikipedia: Potentiality and actuality
  • Plotinos: the One is "the power of all things" (δύναμις πάντων, Enneads III.8.10). In the Gospels dynameis are miracles, deeds of power (Mark 6:2).
  • Latin renders it potentia (potentiality), potestas (authority), and virtus (power, efficacy); Arabic quwwa, so that bi-l-quwwa means "potentially".
  • Modern descendants: dynamic, dynamics, dynamo, dynasty, dynamite (Alfred Nobel, 1867).

Scope Note

Dynamis is not one thing: a mathematical power, a capacity, a potentiality, an authority, and a miracle are all dynamis. In this nomenclature what can be is a matter of CONTINGENCY and the possible, and power in the physicist's sense belongs to kinetics and energy.
