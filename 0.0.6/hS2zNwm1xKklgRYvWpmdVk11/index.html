Definition

Sentience is a practice in service of the norms of flourishing and survival: an organism's use of its senses to select what matters to it from the patterns around it. Its working assumption is the mind-dependent notion of a mind-independent world (MDNMIW).

Sentience selects rather than traces causes. It finds isolates, aggregates, and voids in time and space before it has any word for them, and it is the ground on which every later practice is corroborated.

History Note

  • Jakob von Uexküll: each animal lives in its own Umwelt, the world as its senses select it (Umwelt und Innenwelt der Tiere, 1909). Wikipedia: Umwelt
  • C. I. Lewis distinguishes the given in experience from the concepts by which the mind interprets it (Mind and the World-Order, 1929). Wikipedia: Clarence Irving Lewis
  • Wilfrid Sellars attacks the "Myth of the Given", with Lewis among his targets ("Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind", 1956). Wikipedia: Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind
  • James J. Gibson: what an animal perceives are affordances, what the environment offers it (The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception, 1979). Wikipedia: Affordance

Scope Note

Sentient practice is not discredited by calling it the Myth of the Given. Sentience is not consciousness in general, and it is not a theory of perception; sentience is what the senses are for. It is a performative error to claim one is not involved in sentient practice.
