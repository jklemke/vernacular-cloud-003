Definition

Physis (φύσις) is the Greek word for nature, from phyein, to grow. It names both the growing and what a thing grows into: its character, its constitution, what it is by birth.

The first philosophers wrote books "on nature"; the sophists set physis against nomos, custom and law; Aristotle defined nature as a source of motion and rest within a thing, and set it against technē, art. Herakleitos had already said that nature loves to hide.

History Note

  • The word's one appearance in Homer: Hermes shows Odysseus the physis of the herb moly, its nature, root black and flower white (Odyssey 10.302–306).
  • Many early philosophers' works were later titled Peri physeōs, "On Nature". The sophists argue physis against nomos; Kallikles in Plato's Gorgias claims that by nature the stronger should rule (482e–484c).
  • Aristotle: nature is a principle (archē) of motion and rest in that to which it belongs primarily (Physics II.1, 192b21–23). Wikipedia: Physis
  • Latin renders it natura (from nasci, to be born); Arabic ṭabīʿa. The Metaphysics is named for coming "after the physics" in the edition of Andronikos of Rhodes.
  • Modern descendants: physics, physical, physiology, physician, physique, metaphysics.

Scope Note

Physis is not the physical world of modern physics: it grows, and it has ends (telos). In this nomenclature the Physical Realms are interpretive frameworks, from kinetics to ecology, and what the Greeks called nature is spread across them. The old contrasts survive in new places: nature against art in technē and enaction, nature against custom in sociality.
