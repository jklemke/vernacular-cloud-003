Definition

Absolute Description is the puzzle that reason meets when it asks, "Is there a theory of everything?"

Thesis: a complete description of the world is possible, at least in principle: one set of laws from which every particular follows.

Antithesis: every description selects and omits; there cannot be a generalization that explains all particulars.

It is one of the three antinomies Badinage adds to Kant's four.

Notes

Example

In a story by Borges, the cartographers of an empire draw a map so exact that it is the size of the empire itself. Later generations find it useless and leave it to rot in the desert ("On Exactitude in Science", 1946).

"The theory of everything is a logical contradiction: there cannot be a generalization that explains all particulars."

Victor Badinage

History Note

  • Laplace imagines an intellect that knew every force and every position, for which "nothing would be uncertain and the future, as the past, would be present to its eyes" (Philosophical Essay on Probabilities, 1814).
  • Alfred Korzybski: "A map is not the territory" (Science and Sanity, 1933). Wikipedia: Map–territory relation
  • J. L. Austin names the "descriptive fallacy": the assumption that the business of a statement can only be to describe (How to Do Things with Words, 1962).
  • Sellars and Brandom extend the critique: modal and normative vocabularies do not describe; they make explicit the framework in which describing is possible (Brandom, From Empiricism to Expressivism, 2015).
  • Steven Weinberg's Dreams of a Final Theory (1992) defends the physicist's hope for one theory from which all others follow.

Scope Note

The antinomy arises from descriptivism: the assumption that the business of language is to describe, and that a complete enough description would be the world. To mistake the map for the territory is a near relative of the token illusion. The token illusion works at the level of the word, where a token's identity with itself is taken for the identity of what it names; descriptivism works at the level of the sentence, the theory, and the argument, where the exactitude of a description is taken for the exactitude of what it describes.
