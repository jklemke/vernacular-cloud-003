Definition

Absolute Syntax is the puzzle that reason meets when it asks, "Is there one true logic, one grammar beneath every language?"

Thesis: behind every language lies a single logical form, a syntax in which every truth could be stated and every inference checked.

Antithesis: every syntax is engineered for a purpose, and no syntax fully captures the meanings it manipulates.

It is one of the three antinomies Badinage adds to Kant's four.

Notes

Example

"Colorless green ideas sleep furiously."

Chomsky's example of a sentence that is grammatical and meaningless (Syntactic Structures, 1957)

"Logic and mathematics are syntactical engineering solutions to the problem of building a monotonic, axiomatic, extensional, deductive language."

Victor Badinage

History Note

  • Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) builds a formal language of pure thought, after Leibniz's dream of a universal characteristic. SEP: Frege's Logic
  • Russell and Whitehead try to derive mathematics from logic (Principia Mathematica, 1910–1913); the early Wittgenstein finds the logical form of the world in the form of the proposition (Tractatus, 1921).
  • Gödel shows that any consistent formal system rich enough for arithmetic contains true statements it cannot prove (1931).
  • Quine regiments science into a "canonical notation" of first-order logic (Word and Object, 1960, §33).
  • Newell and Simon's physical symbol system hypothesis holds that symbol manipulation suffices for intelligence (1976); Searle's Chinese room answers that syntax is not sufficient for semantics (1980).

Scope Note

Formal semantics is not semantics: it is meaning-preserving computation, but one must start with a meaning. Logicism hoped that the syntax of logic would carry all of mathematics, and perhaps all of thought; generative grammar hoped that syntax would carry language. Early AI and today's large language models are syntax manipulators at ever greater scale. Whether syntax ever yields meaning is the antinomy in its newest form.
