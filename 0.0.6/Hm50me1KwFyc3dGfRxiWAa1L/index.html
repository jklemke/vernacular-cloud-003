Definition

A discourse is the unit of signification at which sentences are strung together into a coherent whole. It covers everything from a mathematical proof or an academic paper to an advertisement, a gossipy anecdote, a preacher's sermon, a short story, a piece of holy scripture, or an ancient stele. Here meaning is carried by linguistic pragmatics and by the shape of the whole, not by any single sentence.

History Note

  • Aristotle: a thesis is a paradoxical supposition of some eminent philosopher (Top. I.11, 104b19–20).
  • W. V. O. Quine: the unit of empirical significance is the whole of science ("Two Dogmas of Empiricism", 1951, §6). Wikipedia: Two Dogmas of Empiricism
  • H. P. Grice: what a speaker means goes beyond what the sentence says ("Logic and Conversation", 1975). Wikipedia: Implicature
  • Jean-François Lyotard defines the postmodern as incredulity toward grand narratives (La condition postmoderne, 1979). Wikipedia: Metanarrative

Scope Note

A discourse is how we string sentences together to form a coherent narrative. A proof and a fable, a sermon and a stele, are all discourses; the critique of meaning at this level asks what kind of discourse one is reading.
