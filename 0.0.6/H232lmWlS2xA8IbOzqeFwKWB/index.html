Definition

Reality is the norm of corroborative practice. One must presuppose a notion of reality in order to operate in the sentient and sapient realms at all; it gathers ENTIRETY, actuality, existence, world, and universe under one normative category, and it grounds the practice of corroboration.

Notes

Example

Boswell told Dr Johnson that Berkeley's argument for the non-existence of matter could not be refuted. Johnson kicked a large stone until he rebounded from it: "I refute it thus."

"For Kant, the scandal is that philosophy hasn't proved it; but perhaps the scandal is that we think it ought to be provable?"

Victor Badinage

History Note

  • Berkeley: for sensible things, to be is to be perceived, esse est percipi (Principles §3). SEP: George Berkeley
  • Kant calls it "a scandal to philosophy" that the existence of things outside us must be accepted merely on faith (KrV B xxxix note).
  • Peirce defines the real as that whose characters are independent of what anybody may think them to be, and ties it to the opinion that inquiry is fated to reach ("How to Make Our Ideas Clear", 1878).
  • G. E. Moore answers the skeptic by holding up his hands: here is one hand, and here is another ("Proof of an External World", 1939).

Scope Note

Reality is a norm, not a thesis: the nomenclature takes no side between realism and anti-realism, and "realism" is a shifty term (mathematical realism, naive realism). What sentience presupposes is a mind-dependent notion of a mind-independent world. As a logical axiom that looks like a contradiction; as a precondition of sentience, it is simply where we start.
