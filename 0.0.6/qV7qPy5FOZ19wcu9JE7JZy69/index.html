Definition

Metaphor is the mode of signification by which language built on ostension is carried beyond it: the use of a grammatical structure to signify something other than its ostensive referent. Poetical, abstract, allegorical, mythical, and transcendent language all emerge from an ostensive foundation by way of metaphor.

Notes

Example

Lakoff and Johnson's conceptual metaphor ARGUMENT IS WAR: Your claims are indefensible. He attacked every weak point in my argument. No one has been wounded; the structure of combat has been carried over to the structure of debate.

History Note

  • Aristotle: metaphor is giving a thing a name that belongs to something else (Poet. 21, 1457b6–7).
  • Friedrich Nietzsche: truths are metaphors that have been worn out and have lost their sensuous force ("On Truth and Lies in a Nonmoral Sense", 1873). Wikipedia: On Truth and Lies in a Nonmoral Sense
  • Max Black's interaction view: a metaphor brings two systems of commonplaces to bear on each other ("Metaphor", Proc. Arist. Soc., 1954–55).
  • George Lakoff and Mark Johnson: our ordinary conceptual system is fundamentally metaphorical and grounded in bodily experience (Metaphors We Live By, 1980). Wikipedia: Conceptual metaphor

Scope Note

Metaphor is not ornament. A fully grammatical language first lets social creatures refer to actual and possible situations in their shared environment; metaphorical, transcendental, abstract, and mythical language emerge from that ostensive foundation.
