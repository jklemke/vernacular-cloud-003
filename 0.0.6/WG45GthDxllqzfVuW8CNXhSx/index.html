Definition

The norms are the preconditions of practice: presuppositional constraints that govern behaviour and sort good outcomes from bad, better from worse. They are the why behind the why. Normativity begins with life, not with language.

The first norm is survival; without life there are no norms. The others emerge as practice grows: flourishing, reality, beauty, meaning, truth, and legitimacy. Each is the norm of a practice. Giving and asking for reasons is one such practice, in the service of the norm of truth.

Example

"A description is an ought: it ought to describe correctly."

"The answer to Hume is that ought enables is. That is, our descriptions of 'what is' ought to be true."

"Survival is the norm of metabolism. Flourishing is the norm of sentience. Truth is the norm of sapience."

Victor Badinage

History Note

  • Hume observes that writers pass unnoticed from "is" to "ought", and asks how the one can be deduced from the other (Treatise 3.1.1). Wikipedia: Is–ought problem
  • Robert Brandom locates norms in social practice, in the commitments and entitlements of "the game of giving and asking for reasons" (Making It Explicit, 1994).
  • The pragmatists of the Peirce–Lewis line, as Cheryl Misak reads them, treat truth as a regulative norm of inquiry rather than a definition (The American Pragmatists, 2013). SEP: Pragmatism
  • Enactivism finds norms of viability in every living system: an organism's own activity sorts what is good and bad for it (Evan Thompson, Mind in Life, 2007).
  • Terrence Deacon traces the emergence of ends, purposes, and values from non-living chemistry (Incomplete Nature, 2011).

Scope Note

Gravity also sorts outcomes, but it is not a norm. A norm governs a practice, and there is no practice without life. The nomenclature takes the norms to be real, emergent features of living and social systems: supervenient on physics but not reducible to it, and neither linguistic inventions nor mere ways of talking. Norms are metalinguistic, apophatic in some sense: we approach them pragmatically; we do not constitute them by definition.
