Definition

Absolute Beginning is the puzzle that reason meets when it asks, "Why is there something rather than nothing?"

Thesis: the world had a beginning: a first moment, a first cause, a reason why there is something.

Antithesis: the world had no beginning: every moment has a before, every cause a cause, every reason a further reason.

It is Kant's first antinomy.

Notes

Example

Augustine reports an answer to the question "What was God doing before he made heaven and earth?" He was preparing hell for those who pry into such mysteries. Augustine declines the joke and answers that there was no "before": time was made with the world (Conf. XI.12–13).

What came before the big bang?

History Note

  • Aristotle argues that motion and time have no beginning, so the world is eternal (Phys. VIII.1; De Caelo I.10–12); Avicenna makes the world eternally emanate from a necessary being.
  • Al-Ghazali's Incoherence of the Philosophers (1095) opens by refuting the philosophers' doctrine that the world is eternal.
  • Leibniz: "Why is there something rather than nothing?" (Principles of Nature and Grace §7, 1714). SEP: Nothingness
  • Kant's first antinomy: the world has a beginning in time and is limited in space; the world has no beginning and no limits (KrV A426/B454).
  • Georges Lemaître proposes that the universe began from a "primeval atom" (1931), the origin of the big bang theory.

Scope Note

The big bang does not settle the antinomy; it moves it. Beyond the first moment the physicist can describe, the question returns. The Lord's Leap answers it by positing a creator, which turns a logical puzzle into a political gambit. The antinomy is closely tied to time, the first of the Unsayables.
