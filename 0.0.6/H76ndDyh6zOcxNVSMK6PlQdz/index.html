Definition

The Myth of the Haven is the spell that falls on edifying practice when its goal is taken to be a final haven: emancipation, nirvana, salvation, heaven, anagoge, gnosis, wisdom achieved. The haven may be Aristotle's eudaimonia or Socrates' knowledge as virtue; the Gnostic's knowledge and the Sufi's union are havens too. The sage who has reached the haven becomes a maven.

Notes

Example

Plato's prisoner is unchained, dragged up out of the cave, and at last can look at the sun itself. He would rather be a poor man's serf on earth than go back to the shadows, and the rulers of the city must compel him to return (Rep. VII, 514a–520c).

Is it possible to teach to the young the subtle difference between mysticism and hypocrisy? Between gnosticism and dogmatism?

History Note

  • Socrates argues that virtue is knowledge, and so can be taught (Meno 87c–89a; Prot. 352b–c).
  • Aristotle makes the contemplative life the highest eudaimonia, the most divine activity open to a human being (NE X.7). SEP: Aristotle's Ethics
  • The Buddhist path ends in nirvāṇa, the extinction of craving and release from rebirth. Wikipedia: Nirvana
  • The Gnostics teach salvation through secret knowledge (gnōsis); the Sufis seek the passing away of the self in God (fanāʾ).
  • Medieval exegesis reads scripture in four senses, the highest being the anagogical: the sense that leads up to the last things (Dante, Epistle to Cangrande §7).

Scope Note

Edification, Bildung, liberal education, is the practice of forming the young. It cannot do without a haven of some kind, and it is not discredited by calling it the Myth of the Haven. The questions remain: can we teach the young to set aside the exoteric meaning of their culture in order to luxuriate in the esoteric? Can modernism be taught as a kind of piety? If we invest in it the public good, will it allow the young to thrive in the sublime? See edification-piety-emancipation.
